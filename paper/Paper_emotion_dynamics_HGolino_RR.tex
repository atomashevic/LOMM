\begin{abstract}
Automated facial-expression scoring can turn video into dense time
series of visible affect, but these channels are model outputs rather
than direct observations of latent emotion dynamics. Behavioral
applications therefore need simulation frameworks that make the latent
measurement structure known before dynamic methods are interpreted
substantively. We introduce the Latent Oscillator Measurement Model
(LOMM), a generative framework for evaluating dynamic dimensional
recovery in facial-expression-recognition-like video score series. LOMM
generates second-order latent oscillator trajectories, gates their
activity over time, and maps them through a block measurement layer into
noisy observed score panels with known dimensionality, loading strength,
measurement error, feedback regime, and activity pattern. We calibrated
the generator against MAFW-derived score panels using targets for
amplitude dispersion, local movement, temporal persistence,
directional-regime complexity, spectral complexity, and dimensional
spread. The calibrated generator reproduced several local temporal and
dimensional signatures, with a remaining amplitude-dispersion mismatch.
We then compared Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis (DynEGA),
GraphicalVAR, and bounded GIMME in known-truth simulations, including
community-detection choice as a diagnostic factor in the bounded
comparator. DynEGA showed the strongest dimensional recovery and
completed the expanded grid. GraphicalVAR recovered dimensionality in
many conditions but was slower and more sensitive to weak measurement
conditions, consistent with partial-correlation attenuation of
common-cause measurement blocks. GIMME was more flexible but
computationally less feasible in this design. LOMM links generative
modeling, empirical realism checks, and known-truth recovery analysis so
researchers can evaluate whether dynamic methods recover latent
organization, tolerate measurement error, and scale to video-derived
behavioral time series before substantive interpretation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Recent advancements in deep learning and computer vision have made it
increasingly feasible to transform video frames into time series of
emotional expression scores (\citeproc{ref-li2022Deep}{S. Li \& Deng,
2022}; \citeproc{ref-meng2017IdentityAware}{Meng et al., 2017}). In a
facial-expression recognition task, an algorithm maps each image frame
or video segment to emotion-label predictions, usually as category
probabilities or continuous scores for labels such as happiness, anger,
sadness, fear, surprise, disgust, or neutral expression. For
behavioral-science applications, these tools create a new kind of
intensive longitudinal record: high-frequency, model-derived
measurements of visible affective expression. These records differ
substantially from self-report time series collected through ecological
momentary assessment (EMA; \citeproc{ref-tong2005Use}{Tong et al.,
2005}) or experience sampling methods (ESM;
\citeproc{ref-hoemann2020Contextaware}{Hoemann et al., 2020}).
Facial-expression recognition (FER) scores are dense outputs of
measurement systems applied to video frames, making them promising for
studying the temporal organization of emotional expression at scale.
Their use also raises a methodological problem: dynamic models need to
separate latent expressive processes from the observed score series used
to measure them.

The substantive motivation comes from video-rich behavioral settings in
which visible affect is part of the behavior being studied. Facial
expressions are central to human communication
(\citeproc{ref-barrett2019Emotionalb}{Barrett et al., 2019};
\citeproc{ref-mehu2015Emotion}{Mehu \& Scherer, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-stewart2009Presidential}{Stewart et al., 2009}), but many
behavioral-science questions concern how these expressions unfold over
time rather than how a single frame should be labeled. In psychotherapy
tasks, autism assessments, collaborative work, public communication, and
social-media videos, the relevant questions are dynamic: whether
expressions return quickly after perturbation, whether affective
displays settle into stable or volatile regimes, and whether many
model-output score channels reflect fewer latent expressive dimensions
(\citeproc{ref-hollensteinStateSpaceGrids2007}{Hollenstein, 2007};
\citeproc{ref-houbenRelationShorttermEmotion2015}{Houben et al., 2015};
\citeproc{ref-kuppensEmotionDynamics2017}{Kuppens \& Verduyn, 2017}).

Automated facial-analysis systems now make this kind of work feasible at
scale. Datasets such as FER2013
(\citeproc{ref-goodfellow2013Challenges}{Goodfellow et al., 2013})
supported deep learning approaches to facial-expression recognition,
especially Convolutional Neural Networks
(\citeproc{ref-arriaga2017Realtimea}{Arriaga et al., 2017}; CNNs;
\citeproc{ref-ezerceli2022Convolutional}{Ezerceli \& Eskil, 2022}). Most
early systems inherited their emotion categories from the training data
and focused on fixed classification tasks, commonly returning
probabilities for seven categories: anger, disgust, fear, happiness,
sadness, surprise, and neutral facial expression
(\citeproc{ref-wang2022Rethinking}{Wang et al., 2022}). More recently,
transformer-based and CLIP-like models have enabled zero-shot
classification with user-specified emotion labels
(\citeproc{ref-foteinopoulou2023EmoCLIP}{Foteinopoulou \& Patras, 2023};
\citeproc{ref-li2023CLIPER}{H. Li et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-tomasevic2026TransforEmotion}{Tomašević et al., 2026}),
making it possible to represent each video frame as a vector of
emotion-label scores. Applied studies are already using such automated
scoring in clinical, collaborative, and social-media settings
(\citeproc{ref-halamovaAutomatedFacialExpression2023}{Halamová et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-holidayMultimodalEmotionPerspective2023}{Holiday et
al., 2023}; \citeproc{ref-liDecodingGroupEmotional2024}{Q. Li et al.,
2024};
\citeproc{ref-manelisbaramComparingThreeAlgorithms2025}{Manelis-Baram et
al., 2025}).

This technical progress also creates a validation problem. FER-like
score series are model-derived, multivariate, and error-prone
measurements of visible behavior. Their interpretation depends on
whether the observed score channels are treated as emotion variables in
their own right, as indicators of social interaction, or as noisy
indicators of lower-dimensional latent affective processes
(\citeproc{ref-mckeownAreFacialExpression2024}{McKeown \& Allison,
2024}). For dynamic modeling, this distinction has practical
consequences: measurement error can change the structure recovered by
dynamic-network methods, making it important to separate latent temporal
organization from the fallible score series used to observe it
(\citeproc{ref-kankaanpaa2026multipleindicator}{Kankaanpää et al.,
2026}).

The aim of this paper is to introduce the Latent Oscillator Measurement
Model (LOMM), a generative simulation model for FER-like emotion score
series (\citeproc{ref-boker2015Adaptive}{Boker, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-boker2002Method}{Boker \& Nesselroade, 2002};
\citeproc{ref-ollero2023Characterizing}{Ollero et al., 2023}). LOMM
first generates latent expressive trajectories from second-order
oscillator dynamics and then maps those latent processes into FER-like
classifier score outputs through an explicit measurement layer. In this
sense, LOMM is not an empirical estimator of emotion dynamics from a
single video; it is a known-truth data-generating model that lets
researchers control latent dimensionality, feedback regime, loading
strength, measurement error, and activity pattern before testing whether
dynamic methods recover the intended structure.

The paper contributes this model and evaluates its use in three linked
steps, shown in Figure~\ref{fig-pipeline}. First, we specify the LOMM
data-generating and measurement components for FER-like video score
panels. Second, we compare simulated panels with empirical MAFW-derived
FER-like score panels to assess whether the generator reproduces
selected temporal and dimensional signatures (Panels A--D). Third, we
use the resulting known-truth simulations to evaluate dynamic
dimensional recovery with DynEGA, GraphicalVAR, and bounded GIMME under
controlled measurement and dynamical conditions (Panels E--H). We then
close by discussing when video-derived emotion traces can support
behavioral-science claims about latent affective dynamics.

\section{Expressive Affective Dynamics as an Oscillator Measurement
Model}\label{expressive-affective-dynamics-as-an-oscillator-measurement-model}

Affective-dynamics research treats emotion as a time-varying process
rather than as a static score. Across common approaches, the relevant
features include average intensity, variability, instability, inertia,
transitions between states, and return after perturbation
(\citeproc{ref-hollensteinStateSpaceGrids2007}{Hollenstein, 2007};
\citeproc{ref-houbenRelationShorttermEmotion2015}{Houben et al., 2015};
\citeproc{ref-kuppensEmotionDynamics2017}{Kuppens \& Verduyn, 2017}).
For video-derived expressive behavior, the same logic applies to visible
affect. A short clip can contain low expressivity, brief expressive
episodes, gradual recovery, persistent activation, or alternation
between expressive regimes. Modeling expressive affect therefore
requires a representation that can describe both the level of a latent
expressive process and its tendency to persist, change direction, or
return toward equilibrium.

An oscillator model provides a compact way to represent these features
once latent variables are understood as theory-guided expressive
dimensions that organize FER model outputs rather than as the classifier
score labels themselves. Affective structure can be specified in several
defensible ways: core-affect and circumplex models organize affect
around valence and arousal; PAD models add dominance or control; and
Plutchik-style accounts arrange emotion families around opponent
tendencies such as joy--sadness, trust--disgust, fear--anger, and
anticipation--surprise
(\citeproc{ref-plutchikNatureEmotions2001}{Plutchik, 2001};
\citeproc{ref-russellCircumplexModel1980}{Russell, 1980},
\citeproc{ref-russellCoreAffect2003}{2003};
\citeproc{ref-russellMehrabianThreeFactor1977}{Russell \& Mehrabian,
1977}). Facial-expression research uses the same distinction between
category-like outputs and lower-dimensional structure: AffectNet
annotates facial images with both discrete expression categories and
continuous valence-arousal ratings, ABAW evaluates valence-arousal
estimation alongside expression classification and action-unit
detection, and integrated accounts of facial communication treat emotion
categories and dimensions as complementary descriptions
(\citeproc{ref-kolliasABAW2023}{Kollias et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-mehu2015Emotion}{Mehu \& Scherer, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-mollahosseiniAffectNetDatabase2019}{Mollahosseini et al.,
2019}). For a CLIP-like FER panel, this measurement logic can be made
concrete. Frame-level prompt scores for happiness, amusement, trust, or
smiling can be treated as noisy indicators of an approach-affiliation
trajectory; anger, fear, disgust, contempt, or anxiety scores can
indicate a threat-defense trajectory; and surprise, anticipation,
confusion, or attention scores can indicate an orienting-novelty
trajectory. LOMM generates the three latent trajectories first, maps
them through block loadings into classifier-output score channels, and
then lets the recovery method operate only on the simulated score
matrix. The known-truth question is therefore whether a method such as
DynEGA can recover the intended expressive blocks from CLIP-FER-like
outputs whose labels are model scores rather than direct observations of
emotion. The model has two integrated parts: a latent oscillator process
for expressive dynamics and a measurement model adapted from the direct
autoregressive factor score model (\citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{H.
Golino et al., 2022}; \citeproc{ref-nesselroade2013dynamic}{Nesselroade
et al., 2013}).

Following Ollero et al. (\citeproc{ref-ollero2023Characterizing}{2023}),
LOMM treats each latent expressive dimension as a continuous-time
second-order state process. Let \(i=1,\ldots,V\) index videos, \(t\)
index time, and \(f=1,\ldots,m\) index the latent expressive dimensions
selected for a simulation. For video \(i\), \(x_{if}(t)\) is the latent
level of dimension \(f\), and \(v_{if}(t)=\dot{x}_{if}(t)\) is its
instantaneous rate of change. The implemented latent process is

\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d} t}
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{if}(t) \\
v_{if}(t)
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
\eta_f & \zeta_f
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{if}(t) \\
v_{if}(t)
\end{bmatrix}
+
\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
q_{if}(t)
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
q_{if}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{qf}^{2}),
\label{eq:lomm-latent}
\end{equation}

or equivalently,
\(\ddot{x}_{if}(t)=\eta_f x_{if}(t)+\zeta_f \dot{x}_{if}(t)+q_{if}(t)\).
This is an uncoupled multivariate system: \(\eta_f\), \(\zeta_f\), and
\(\sigma_{qf}\) may vary by factor, but the model used here does not
include off-diagonal dynamic effects in which one latent expressive
dimension directly changes the acceleration of another. We therefore
interpret \(\eta_f\) as position feedback and \(\zeta_f\) as velocity
feedback. Under this sign convention, negative \(\eta_f\) pulls a
displaced latent trajectory back toward its equilibrium level; stronger
negative values imply stronger restoring acceleration. Negative
\(\zeta_f\) attenuates velocity over time, \(\zeta_f=0\) removes
velocity feedback, and positive \(\zeta_f\) can sustain or amplify
ongoing change over the finite interval being simulated. The innovation
\(q_{if}(t)\) is a factor-specific shock process that perturbs the
latent trajectory. In the simulations reported here, the continuous-time
system is advanced with the exact linear transition matrix and
initialized with \(x_{if}(1)=0.25\) and \(v_{if}(1)=2\).

For simulation-based evaluation, LOMM defines scenario conditions at two
levels. First, the factor-specific parameters \(\eta_f\), \(\zeta_f\),
and \(\sigma_{qf}\) set the dynamical regime: negative velocity feedback
produces damped return, zero velocity feedback removes damping, and
positive velocity feedback can sustain or amplify change over the finite
simulated interval. Second, time-varying activity weights determine
which latent dimension dominates the generated score series. In
single-regime scenarios, one dimension has higher activity across the
full sequence; in switching scenarios, the active dimension changes
after sampled dwell intervals; and in low-expression scenarios, all
dimensions remain close to background activity. Formally, the trajectory
passed to the measurement model is
\(\tilde{x}_{if}(t)=a_{if}(t)x_{if}(t)\), where \(a_{if}(t)\) is a
scenario-defined activity weight. This design lets the simulation vary
damping, dominance, switching, and volatility while keeping the
source-of-truth latent dimensionality and measurement blocks known.

The observable FER-like score vector is generated from the
scenario-scaled latent trajectories through a block measurement model.
For video \(i\), if each latent dimension has \(k\) observed indicators,
then \(p=mk\) and

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{y}_{it}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_i\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{it}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{it},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{it}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_i).
\label{eq:lomm-measurement}
\end{equation}

Each observed score loads on exactly one latent dimension: for indicator
\(j\) in block \(B_f\), \((\Lambda_i)_{jf}=\lambda+u_{ij}\) with
\(u_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-0.15,0.15)\), and all off-block loadings are
zero. The measurement-error covariance matrix is diagonal,
\(\mathbf{\Sigma}_i=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma^2_{\varepsilon,i1},\ldots,\sigma^2_{\varepsilon,ip})\),
with \(\sigma^2_{\varepsilon,ij}=s^2_{\varepsilon}+r_{ij}\) and
\(r_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-0.01,0.01)\). In the simulation grid,
\(s_{\varepsilon}\) is reported as the measurement-error standard
deviation, and \(s^2_{\varepsilon}\) enters the diagonal covariance
matrix. Thus LOMM simulates measured expressive factors and their
observed indicator blocks; neutrality is not part of the source-truth
structure in the simulation.

For the empirical realism benchmark, this linear block predictor is
transformed to the observed score scale with a row-wise softmax link. If
\(\mathbf{z}_{it}=\mathbf{\Lambda}_i\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{it}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{it}\)
is the pre-link score vector, then the benchmarked observation layer
uses

\[
y_{itj}=\frac{\exp(z_{itj})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{p}\exp(z_{it\ell})},
\]

equivalent to a softmax temperature of 1.00. No empirical log-mean
channel intercept and no additional neutral or background denominator
are added in the retained realism benchmark. The softmax link is used to
put the empirical realism panels on a bounded, compositional score
scale; the known-truth recovery target remains the latent block
structure that generated the pre-link score channels.

As a concrete three-factor example, a Plutchik-informed specification
could set \(m=3\) for affiliative/approach expression, threat/defensive
expression, and orienting/novelty expression
(\citeproc{ref-plutchikNatureEmotions2001}{Plutchik, 2001}). FER-like
indicators related to joy, trust, amusement, or smiling would form one
block; indicators related to anger, fear, or disgust would form a second
block; and surprise- or anticipation-related indicators would form a
third. This specification illustrates the measurement logic of LOMM: the
researcher states a theory-guided latent organization, the simulator
generates known latent trajectories under that organization, and the
classifier-output score channels are noisy indicators whose loading
strength and measurement error are substantive simulation conditions
(\citeproc{ref-kankaanpaa2026multipleindicator}{Kankaanpää et al.,
2026}). Alternative affective taxonomies can be encoded by changing the
latent block map.

The same three-factor example is shown in Figure~\ref{fig-dlosim} to
make the role of the latent shock term visible.

}

\caption{\label{fig-dlosim}Three-factor LOMM example for
Plutchik-informed latent expressive dimensions. The left panel shows
smooth reference trajectories without dynamic shocks; the right panel

process with factor-specific position feedback, velocity feedback, and

Figure~\ref{fig-dlosim} highlights the role of the innovation variance,
\(\sigma_q^2\), in LOMM. The reference panel shows the conceptual latent
ordering without dynamic shocks: affiliative expression rises quickly
and settles above the threat and orienting trajectories. The simulated
oscillator panel keeps this same structure, but nonzero factor-specific
\(\sigma_q\) perturbs each latent process at each time point, producing
local peaks, reversals, and irregular fluctuations around the underlying
trajectory. After approximately time point 100, affiliative expression
(blue) remains the dominant latent tendency, while threat (orange)
fluctuates at a moderate level and orienting (green) stays lower with
shorter local excursions. Thus, \(\sigma_q\) is the mechanism that turns
a smooth conceptual model into latent score series that better resemble
the uneven temporal structure of FER-like video data, without adding a
neutrality trajectory or cross-factor coupling.

\section{Method}\label{method}

The analyses follow the workflow in Figure~\ref{fig-pipeline}. We first
use empirical video scores to define realism targets for FER-like score
series (Panels A--C). We then translate those targets into LOMM scenario
conditions (Panel D) and evaluate dimensional-recovery methods on
simulated panels for which the latent dimensional structure and
measurement blocks are known (Panels E--H).

\subsection{Empirical Calibration and Realism
Targets}\label{empirical-calibration-and-realism-targets}

Figure~\ref{fig-mafwvideo} shows the empirical scoring target that
motivates the simulation study. The figure uses one clip from MAFW, a
dataset designed for dynamic facial expression recognition in the wild
(\citeproc{ref-liu_mafw_2022}{Liu et al., 2022}), whereas the realism
check uses a 100-clip calibration sample scored with the same zero-shot
FER-like procedure. We retrieved frame-level emotion scores with the
transforEmotion R package
(\citeproc{ref-tomasevic2026TransforEmotion}{Tomašević et al., 2026}).
Each sampled video was decoded into frames, and each frame was evaluated
against user-specified emotion labels with the package's
\texttt{oai-large} vision model, which maps to OpenAI CLIP ViT-Large/14
(\texttt{openai/clip-vit-large-patch14})
(\citeproc{ref-radford2021LearningTransferable}{Radford et al., 2021}).
The scoring step returns one frame-level score vector over the
prompt-defined channels used here: anger, disgust, fear, happiness,
neutral, sadness, surprise, contempt, anxiety, helplessness, and
disappointment. Within each clip, short missing runs are interpolated,
frames with complete usable scores are retained, and clipped score
vectors are renormalized so that frame-level channels can be compared as
probability-like outputs. These prompt-specific classifier outputs
provide empirical targets for the LOMM observation layer through their
temporal persistence, variability, and co-fluctuation.

The selected still frames from the same MAFW clip anchor the empirical
example (Panel A). The corresponding score profiles illustrate the
measurement problem (Panel B): different frames receive different scores
across labels such as helplessness, fear, disgust, sadness, anxiety, and
disappointment. The frame-level trajectories show that the highest mean
channels change in bursts rather than as smooth curves (Panel C). The
single-video DynEGA network applies Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis
to the same clip's non-neutral score trajectories (Panel D). In this
illustration, the method returns three communities among ten score
channels, motivating the dimensional-recovery problem later studied
under known truth.

The empirical calibration sample was assembled at the clip level from a
label-diverse MAFW manifest and then retained after frame-score quality
control. The retained panel contains 100 clips spanning the 11 MAFW
single-label categories. After interpolation of short missing runs and
complete-row filtering, usable clip lengths ranged from 17 to 50 frames,
with a median of 36.5 usable frames. These retained frame-score panels
define the empirical distributions used for the realism targets.

For the empirical realism check, empirical and simulated panels are
summarized at two levels. Observable-channel metrics are computed
directly from the FER-like score series within each clip.
Community-score metrics are computed after applying pooled DynEGA to the
score panel and extracting score summaries for the recovered
communities. This distinction matters because a simulated panel can
approximate the observable score channels while still producing a
different lower-dimensional geometry, or conversely match the coarse
latent organization while retaining imperfect channel-level amplitude
behavior.

The primary realism targets include channel-level IQR, derivative IQR,
temporal complexity, and dimensional spread. Channel IQR indexes score
dispersion; derivative IQR indexes the typical scale of local
frame-to-frame movement; lag-1 autocorrelation indexes local
persistence; run-length entropy indexes directional-regime complexity;
spectral entropy indexes spectral complexity; and effective rank indexes
multivariate dimensional spread. For each metric, empirical and
simulated distributions are compared using a scaled median difference
and a scaled one-dimensional Wasserstein distance, with the larger
discrepancy used for classification. We use the same threshold rule
across metrics: Pass for scaled discrepancy \(\leq 0.33\), Partial for
scaled discrepancy \(\leq 1.00\), and Fail for scaled discrepancy
\(> 1.00\). These criteria are simulation-construction checks, not
external validation of the scoring model or its emotion labels.

\subsection{Scenario Selection}\label{scenario-selection}

Scenario selection links the empirical calibration targets to the
simulation design. We group the manipulated conditions into three
families (Panel D). The dynamics family varies the position feedback,
velocity feedback, and innovation variance parameters, \(\eta_f\),
\(\zeta_f\), and \(\sigma_{qf}\). The activity family varies whether one
latent expressive dimension dominates across the full sequence, whether
dominance switches after sampled dwell intervals, or whether all
dimensions remain close to low-expression background activity. The
measurement family varies the block loading matrix
\(\mathbf{\Lambda}_i\) and measurement-error covariance matrix
\(\mathbf{\Sigma}_i\) that generate the observed FER-like score panels.

The primary emotion-dynamics simulation uses six scenario families
crossed with two-, three-, and four-dimensional latent source
structures. Four families represent generic expression regimes:
low-expression single dominance, high-expression single dominance,
high-expression switching, and high-expression fast-switching
volatility. Two additional families use the empirical calibration logic
more directly, one with ranked persistent dynamics and one with broader
switching geometry. In all cases, the number of observed channels is
\(p=mk\), where \(m\) is the number of latent dimensions and \(k\) is
the number of observed indicators per dimension. The primary design uses
\(N=50\) or \(100\) videos, \(T=100\) time points, \(k=4\) observed
indicators per latent dimension, measurement-error standard deviations
of \(0.125\), \(0.25\), and \(0.50\), loadings of \(0.4\), \(0.6\), and
\(0.8\), and three replications per structural cell. Two sensitivity
conditions hold the signal settings fixed while increasing the number of
indicators to \(k=6\) or the sequence length to \(T=300\).

For each simulated video, LOMM first generates latent trajectories from
the scenario-specific dynamic parameters. The activity schedule then
scales those trajectories: non-switching scenarios keep one active
dimension for the full sequence, rotating the dominant dimension across
videos, whereas switching scenarios sample dwell intervals and change
the active dimension over time. The measurement layer is applied to the
activity-scaled latent trajectories. Measurement error is reported as a
standard deviation, with the corresponding variance entering the
diagonal measurement-error covariance before small item-level variance
jitter is added. The simulation design evaluates dimensional recovery
under controlled combinations of dynamics, activity regimes, and
measurement quality.

\subsection{Dimensional-Recovery Simulation
Study}\label{dimensional-recovery-simulation-study}

The simulation begins with latent trajectories and the known number of
latent expressive dimensions (Panel E). The LOMM observation layer maps
each observed score channel to one latent dimension through the block
loading matrix and adds item-level measurement error (Panel F),
producing a simulated FER-like score panel (Panel G). The final step is
the dimensional-recovery test, where the estimated number of dimensions
and item memberships are compared against the known latent structure
(Panel H).

We compare Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis {[}DynEGA; H. Golino et
al. (\citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{2022}){]} with GraphicalVAR
(\citeproc{ref-epskampggm}{Epskamp et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-graphicalVAR2024}{Epskamp, 2024}) and GIMME
(\citeproc{ref-gates2012group}{Gates \& Molenaar, 2012};
\citeproc{ref-lane2019uncovering}{Lane et al., 2019}) because they
represent adjacent but distinct approaches to intensive longitudinal
structure. DynEGA is the focal dimensional-recovery method: it estimates
groups of variables whose changes are coordinated over time.
GraphicalVAR is a dynamic-network comparator that separates lagged
temporal relations from contemporaneous partial associations. GIMME is
an idiographic time-series framework that searches for relations that
are common across individuals, shared by subgroups, or unique to
individuals. In the present simulation, DynEGA tests whether a method
designed for dimensional recovery can recover the known score blocks
directly, GraphicalVAR tests whether a dynamic-network representation
yields a similar partition after community detection, and GIMME tests
whether a more flexible person-specific time-series framework is
feasible under the same measurement conditions.

DynEGA combines time-delay embedding and derivative estimation from
dynamical systems with exploratory graph analysis for dimensionality
assessment (\citeproc{ref-glla2010}{Boker et al., 2010};
\citeproc{ref-golino2019investigating}{H. Golino et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-golino2017ega1}{H. F. Golino \& Epskamp, 2017}). We apply
DynEGA to the stacked video panel with video identity supplied as the
grouping variable. The embedding dimension is selected by minimizing the
Total Entropy Fit Index (TEFI) over candidate values
(\citeproc{ref-golino2019entropy}{H. Golino et al., 2021}), using
\(n_\mathrm{embed}\in\{5,10,15,\lfloor T/4\rfloor,\lfloor T/2\rfloor\}\)
where admissible. The estimated DynEGA population network is then
partitioned into communities, which are interpreted as recovered groups
of score channels. The expanded DynEGA grid uses Louvain community
detection (\citeproc{ref-blondel2008fast}{Blondel et al., 2008}) with
the expand rule for unidimensional solutions. In the bounded all-method
comparator, community detection is analyzed as a factor with Louvain,
Leiden (\citeproc{ref-traag2019leiden}{Traag et al., 2019}), and
Walktrap (\citeproc{ref-pons2006walktrap}{Pons \& Latapy, 2005}),
holding the unidimensional rule at expand.

GraphicalVAR estimates a graphical vector autoregressive model for
intensive longitudinal data (\citeproc{ref-epskampggm}{Epskamp et al.,
2018}; \citeproc{ref-graphicalVAR2024}{Epskamp, 2024}). In this
framework, the temporal network contains directed lag-1 regression
parameters, whereas the contemporaneous network contains partial
correlations among residuals at the same time point after lagged effects
have been taken into account. In multilevel applications, the model can
also distinguish within-person temporal and contemporaneous structure
from between-person associations among stationary means. We fit
GraphicalVAR to the stacked simulated score panel and use the estimated
contemporaneous partial-correlation matrix as the structural matrix for
dimensional partitioning, because the LOMM recovery target is an
undirected grouping of score channels rather than a directed
lagged-effect graph. The diagonal is ignored and the resulting weighted
matrix is treated as an undirected graph. Positive edge weights define
the graph, with absolute weights used only when no positive edges are
available. Louvain, Leiden, and Walktrap are applied to the same fitted
contemporaneous structure and are analyzed as the community-detection
factor.

GIMME is an automated person-specific time-series modeling approach
built around unified structural equation modeling and vector
autoregressive representations (\citeproc{ref-gates2012group}{Gates \&
Molenaar, 2012}; \citeproc{ref-lane2019uncovering}{Lane et al., 2019}).
The algorithm searches for lagged and contemporaneous paths that improve
fit across the sample, retains paths that are common to many
individuals, and then allows subgroup-shared or individual-specific
paths so that heterogeneous time-series structures do not have to be
forced into one pooled model. In this simulation, each video is treated
as the idiographic time series supplied to GIMME. We fit the VAR
specification to the video-level score panels, extract the
contemporaneous block from the person-specific \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}\)
matrices, and average those blocks across videos to obtain a common
structural matrix for dimensional partitioning. The diagonal is ignored
and the averaged matrix is treated as an undirected graph. As with
GraphicalVAR, positive edge weights define the graph, with absolute
weights used only when no positive edges are available. Louvain, Leiden,
and Walktrap are applied to the same fitted GIMME structure and are
analyzed as the community-detection factor.

All three methods are evaluated as dimensional-recovery estimators under
known truth. DynEGA is evaluated on an expanded grid for scalability and
on the bounded all-method comparator; GraphicalVAR and GIMME are
evaluated on the bounded comparator because complete factorial
estimation is computationally unstable in this setting.
Community-detection algorithm is included as a diagnostic factor in the
bounded comparator rather than as the primary organizing variable.
Performance is summarized using dimensional hit rate, assignment
accuracy for observed score channels, matched-score correlation,
matched-score normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE), runtime, and
failure or timeout accounting. Dimensional hit rate is the proportion of
method-readout rows in which the method returns the correct number of
latent dimensions. Assignment accuracy asks whether observed score
channels are assigned to the correct latent blocks after optimal
matching of estimated communities to true blocks. For score-based
metrics, observed variables are standardized within the full panel and
community scores are computed from the first principal component of each
estimated block, with row means used when a principal-component score
cannot be estimated. Matched-score correlation and matched-score NRMSE
then compare these recovered score summaries with the known latent
trajectories after sign-invariant matching between estimated communities
and source dimensions. Runtime is recorded for each model fit.
Non-converged fits, post-estimation extraction errors, and predefined
timeouts remain in the denominators. For GraphicalVAR and GIMME, the
same fitted structure is partitioned by multiple community-detection
algorithms, so feasibility is also reported as unique structure-fit
status counts.

The final simulation uses a split design. The expanded DynEGA grid
varies the number of videos, series length, latent dimensionality,
indicators per dimension, loading strength, and measurement-error
standard deviation across 1,296 structural cells with 400 replications,
yielding 518,400 DynEGA Louvain/expand method rows. The all-method
comparator uses the bounded NVAR = 4 design with two- and
three-dimensional source structures, two sample sizes, TimeP = 100,
three loading strengths, three measurement-error standard deviations,
and all six scenario families. This bounded comparator contains 216
structural cells with 100 replications. Louvain, Leiden, and Walktrap
are included as community-detection levels, yielding 64,800
method-readout rows per method in the final comparator. Timeouts, failed
fits, and post-estimation extraction errors are retained in the
reporting denominator because they are part of method behavior in
applied simulation workflows.

\section{Results}\label{results}

Results are organized around four questions that follow the workflow in
Figure~\ref{fig-pipeline}. We first ask whether the calibrated LOMM
generator produces FER-like score panels with empirical temporal and
dimensional signatures. We then evaluate whether dimensional-recovery
methods recover the known latent structure, examine how recovery changes
with measurement quality, and report the computational feasibility of
the compared methods.

\subsection{Empirical Realism Results}\label{empirical-realism-results}

Before evaluating dimensional-recovery methods, we asked whether the
calibrated LOMM generator produced FER-like score panels that were
sufficiently close to empirical panels for simulation construction.
Figure~\ref{fig-empirical-realism} summarizes this check at two readout
levels: the observed score channels and the lower-dimensional community
scores extracted from the pooled DynEGA solution. The comparison
evaluates selected distributional, temporal, spectral, and dimensional
signatures of the empirical MAFW score panels, providing a
simulation-construction check on the simulated panels.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/figure4_empirical_realism.pdf}

}

\caption{\label{fig-empirical-realism}Empirical realism check for
LOMM-generated FER-like panels. Panel A shows pass, partial, and fail
classifications for the six realism targets at two readout levels:
observed score channels and DynEGA community scores. Values are the

Panel B shows the two discrepancy components separately; dashed

1.00.}

At the observed-channel level, the softmax-linked generator passed the
derivative-IQR and run-length entropy checks. Lag-1 autocorrelation,
spectral entropy, and effective rank were partial matches, indicating
that the simulated panels were close to the empirical panels in local
persistence, spectral complexity, and multivariate dimensional spread
but not within the strictest threshold. The only failed observed-channel
check was channel IQR, showing that the simulated observed channels
remained more dispersed than the empirical score channels.

The community-score readout showed the same broad pattern with stronger
amplitude mismatch. Run-length entropy passed, derivative IQR, lag-1
autocorrelation, spectral entropy, and effective rank were partial
matches, and channel IQR failed. The retained Louvain community readout
returned three communities with the same 6-3-2 channel-size pattern in
the empirical and simulated panels, so the coarse dimensional
organization matched in this readout even though the marginal amplitude
of the community scores was too large. Taken together, these results
support using the softmax-linked generator for dimensional-recovery
simulation while keeping the amplitude and dispersion mismatch explicit
as a limitation of the current calibration.

limited scalability across the full LOMM grid.

DynEGA was substantially faster than the two model-based comparators in

expanded DynEGA Louvain/expand grid also completed all 518,400 method

these feasibility counts are unique structure-fit counts rather than
repeated CD readout rows. The final simulation results therefore support
two linked conclusions: DynEGA had the strongest dimensional-recovery
profile in the bounded comparator and was the only method light enough
to cover the expanded LOMM grid; GraphicalVAR and GIMME provide
informative bounded comparators whose interpretation must include
timeout and extraction-error behavior.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

This study introduced LOMM as a known-truth framework for studying
dimensional recovery in FER-like video score series. The strongest
conceptual result is that LOMM turns dynamic dimensional recovery into a
controlled perturbation problem. In the population design, \(m\) latent
oscillator processes generate \(m\) measured score blocks. Within a
block, observed channels share the same latent level and rate of change
up to loading differences, activity weights, and measurement error. The
recovery task is therefore to recover \(m\) rank-one dynamic subspaces
from noisy, activity-gated, block-measured oscillator traces.

This framing clarifies why the method comparison is mainly about target
alignment and scalability. DynEGA is designed to estimate groups of
variables whose changes are coordinated over time, and this target is
close to the population object generated by LOMM. In the ideal case,
each score block forms a coherent dynamic subspace; finite samples,
measurement error, weak loadings, activity switches, and oscillator
amplitude differences perturb that block structure. DynEGA's high
dimensional hit rate, high block-assignment accuracy, and complete
expanded-grid coverage show that the estimated dynamic communities
usually remained close enough to the known LOMM blocks for recovery,
while the low-signal scenarios identify the boundary of that
performance. The community-detection diagnostic did not materially
change this conclusion because its effect on recovery and score-quality
outcomes was negligible relative to method, scenario family, and
measurement quality.

GraphicalVAR is a useful comparator because it estimates dynamic network
structure, but its structural matrix is less directly aligned with a
reflective block measurement model. The contemporaneous
partial-correlation representation conditions away shared variance among
indicators. Under a common-cause block structure, this can attenuate the
within-block edges that define the latent measurement block, even when
the marginal block signal is strong. This population alignment explains
why finite-sample noise, lower loadings, higher measurement error, and
temporal-model estimation can more easily disrupt the recovered
partition. In the final bounded results, community-detection choice made
very little difference once the GraphicalVAR structure fit had been
obtained; the main limits were scenario sensitivity and OpenAI-style
three-dimensional timeouts.

GIMME addresses a broader problem than the one LOMM makes primary. It
can represent group-level, subgroup-level, and individual-level
time-series structure, which makes it attractive for idiographic dynamic
modeling. In this design, that flexibility came with weak
status-inclusive dimensional hit rates, long runtimes, frequent
timeouts, and post-estimation errors in the OpenAI-style panels. GIMME
therefore contributes mainly as a feasibility-stress comparator for this
simulation rather than as a scalable full-grid peer.

The score-recovery results reinforce the same interpretation. Once a
method recovered the correct block structure, recovered score summaries
often tracked the known latent trajectories well. This is expected
because a correctly recovered score block has an oracle-like reliability
determined mainly by loading strength, measurement error, number of
indicators, and latent amplitude. The larger methodological difference
was the probability of reaching the correct structure in the first place
and producing a usable fitted structure within the time limit. In this
sense, method performance in LOMM decomposes into structural recovery
probability, conditional score reliability, and feasibility, with
DynEGA's advantage coming primarily from the first and third components.

The empirical realism check sets the boundary on this interpretation.
The softmax-linked simulated panels matched the empirical FER-like score
panels well for local movement scale and directional-regime texture, and
they partially matched persistence, spectral complexity, and dimensional
spread. Channel IQR failed at both the observed-channel and
community-score readouts, showing that the current calibration still
produces larger marginal amplitude dispersion than the empirical panels.
This mismatch is informative rather than disqualifying: LOMM can
reproduce selected dynamic and dimensional signatures while retaining a
narrower score-range and occupancy limitation in the bounded,
probability-like observation layer.

Several limitations follow directly from this design. FER-like scores
remain model-derived observations of visible expression, so the realism
check evaluates selected statistical signatures rather than the validity
of emotion labels or exact empirical trajectories. The present LOMM
implementation uses independent oscillator dimensions, activity-gated
block loadings, and diagonal measurement error; it does not claim to
capture the full geometry of real affective systems. Future work should
test richer measurement structures, bounded or compositional observation
layers, additional empirical scoring systems, and empirical applications
where known-truth simulation results guide substantive validation of
video-derived affective dynamics.

\section{Appendix A: Population Perturbation View of LOMM
Recovery}\label{appendix-a-population-perturbation-view-of-lomm-recovery}

This appendix formalizes the population argument behind the Discussion.
The result is not a theorem about the fitted DynEGA algorithm, its
community-detection implementation, or the exact behavior of
GraphicalVAR or GIMME software. It is a theorem about the simplified
LOMM population object that any dynamic dimensional-recovery method
receives: observed score channels in the same latent block share
coordinated changes because they are noisy indicators of the same
activity-scaled oscillator.

\subsection{A.1 Simplified Population
LOMM}\label{a.1-simplified-population-lomm}

Consider a population LOMM with \(m\) latent dimensions, \(k\) observed
indicators per dimension, and \(p=mk\) observed score channels. Let
\(B_1,\ldots,B_m\) denote the true indicator blocks. For indicator
\(j \in B_f\), assume the equal-loading, equal-error measurement model

\[
Y_j(t)=\lambda Z_f(t)+\varepsilon_j(t),
\]

where

\[
Z_f(t)=a_f(t)X_f(t)
\]

is the activity-scaled latent oscillator for dimension \(f\),
\(\lambda>0\), and \(\varepsilon_j(t)\) has mean zero and variance
\(\psi=s_\varepsilon^2\). Measurement errors are independent across
indicators and time, and the centered latent processes \(Z_f\) are
mutually independent with finite variance. The implemented simulation
allows loading jitter and item-level error jitter, but this symmetric
case isolates the block geometry that defines the known-truth recovery
target.

\subsection{A.2 Dynamic Coordination
Graph}\label{a.2-dynamic-coordination-graph}

Define the lag-\(h\) score change as

\[
\Delta_hY_j(t)=Y_j(t+h)-Y_j(t),
\]

and define a population dynamic coordination graph by

\[
W^\Delta_{j\ell}
=
\left|\operatorname{Cor}(\Delta_hY_j,\Delta_hY_\ell)\right|.
\]

For two distinct indicators \(j,\ell \in B_f\),

\[
\Delta_hY_j(t)=\lambda \Delta_hZ_f(t)+\Delta_h\varepsilon_j(t),
\]

so

\[
\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta_hY_j,\Delta_hY_\ell)
=
\lambda^2\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_hZ_f)
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_hY_j)
=
\lambda^2\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_hZ_f)+2\psi.
\]

Therefore the within-block dynamic edge is

\[
w^\Delta_f
=
\frac{\theta^\Delta_f}{1+\theta^\Delta_f},
\qquad
\theta^\Delta_f
=
\frac{\lambda^2\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_hZ_f)}{2\psi}.
\]

For \(j \in B_f\) and \(\ell \in B_g\) with \(f \neq g\), independence
of the latent processes and measurement errors gives zero cross-block
covariance. Thus the population dynamic coordination graph has
block-clique form:

\[
W^\Delta
=
\operatorname{blockdiag}
\left(
w^\Delta_1(J_k-I_k),\ldots,w^\Delta_m(J_k-I_k)
\right).
\]

This is the population object most directly aligned with dynamic
dimensional recovery: variables in the same block share the same latent
change signal, while variables in different blocks do not.

\subsection{A.3 Partial-Correlation
Graph}\label{a.3-partial-correlation-graph}

The GraphicalVAR comparator is converted into dimensional partitions by
applying community detection to a contemporaneous partial-correlation
matrix. To see how this representation changes the same block structure,
consider the population covariance matrix inside block \(B_f\). Let

\[
S_f=\operatorname{Var}(Z_f),
\qquad
\theta_f=\frac{\lambda^2S_f}{\psi}.
\]

The block covariance matrix is

\[
\Sigma_f
=
\lambda^2S_f\mathbf{1}_k\mathbf{1}_k^\top+\psi I_k
=
\psi(I_k+\theta_f\mathbf{1}_k\mathbf{1}_k^\top).
\]

Using the Sherman-Morrison identity,

\[
\Sigma_f^{-1}
=
\psi^{-1}
\left[
I_k-
\frac{\theta_f}{1+k\theta_f}
\mathbf{1}_k\mathbf{1}_k^\top
\right].
\]

The off-diagonal precision entry is

\[
(\Sigma_f^{-1})_{j\ell}
=
-\psi^{-1}\frac{\theta_f}{1+k\theta_f},
\qquad j\neq \ell,
\]

and the diagonal precision entry is

\[
(\Sigma_f^{-1})_{jj}
=
\psi^{-1}\frac{1+(k-1)\theta_f}{1+k\theta_f}.
\]

The absolute partial correlation between two indicators in the same
block is therefore

\[
w^P_f
=
\frac{\theta_f}{1+(k-1)\theta_f}.
\]

Because the full covariance matrix is block diagonal under the
simplified population model, cross-block partial correlations are zero.
The GraphicalVAR-style population graph preserves the ideal block
support,

\[
W^P
=
\operatorname{blockdiag}
\left(
w^P_1(J_k-I_k),\ldots,w^P_m(J_k-I_k)
\right),
\]

but compresses the within-block signal. The corresponding marginal
common-source edge would be

\[
w^M_f
=
\frac{\theta_f}{1+\theta_f}.
\]

For the same signal-to-noise ratio \(\theta\),

\[
\frac{w^P(\theta)}{w^M(\theta)}
=
\frac{1+\theta}{1+(k-1)\theta}.
\]

When \(k>2\) and \(\theta>0\), \(w^P(\theta)<w^M(\theta)\). As
\(\theta \to \infty\), \(w^P(\theta) \to 1/(k-1)\), whereas
\(w^M(\theta)\to 1\). Thus partial correlation preserves the ideal block
support but attenuates the shared-source signal that defines a
reflective measurement block.

\subsection{A.4 Perturbation Margin}\label{a.4-perturbation-margin}

The preceding graphs are population graphs. Estimated graphs can be
written as

\[
\widehat W = W + E,
\]

where \(E\) includes sampling error, derivative-estimation error,
model-estimation error, finite-\(T\) and finite-\(N\) error,
regularization, convergence behavior, and other implementation-specific
perturbations. For a block-clique population graph

\[
W=
\operatorname{blockdiag}
\left(
w_1(J_k-I_k),\ldots,w_m(J_k-I_k)
\right),
\]

let \(w_{\min}=\min_f w_f\). Suppose every off-diagonal edge is
estimated within \(\delta\) of its population value:

\[
\max_{j\neq \ell}|\widehat W_{j\ell}-W_{j\ell}|\leq \delta.
\]

For any node \(j \in B_f\), the estimated total connection to its true
block is at least

\[
(k-1)(w_f-\delta),
\]

whereas its estimated total connection to any wrong block is at most

\[
k\delta.
\]

Thus each node has stronger total connection to its true block than to
any wrong block whenever

\[
(k-1)(w_{\min}-\delta)>k\delta,
\]

or equivalently

\[
\delta
<
\frac{k-1}{2k-1}w_{\min}.
\]

This is the elementary perturbation statement. Recovery is favored when
the estimation perturbation is small relative to the weakest
within-block signal. Because the partial-correlation edge
\(w^P(\theta)\) is smaller than the marginal shared-source edge
\(w^M(\theta)\) for \(k>2\), the same perturbation has less margin under
a partial-correlation representation.

\subsection{A.5 Link to Oscillator
Parameters}\label{a.5-link-to-oscillator-parameters}

The signal-to-noise ratios above become LOMM-specific when
\(Z_f(t)=a_f(t)X_f(t)\) is tied to the latent oscillator. In the stable
continuous-time case with constant activity \(a_f\),

\[
\ddot X_f(t)=\eta_fX_f(t)+\zeta_f\dot X_f(t)+q_f(t),
\qquad
\eta_f<0,\ \zeta_f<0.
\]

Under the stationary linear approximation,

\[
\operatorname{Var}(X_f)
=
\frac{\sigma_{qf}^2}{2\eta_f\zeta_f},
\]

which is positive because both \(\eta_f\) and \(\zeta_f\) are negative.
Therefore

\[
\operatorname{Var}(Z_f)
=
a_f^2\operatorname{Var}(X_f)
=
a_f^2\frac{\sigma_{qf}^2}{2\eta_f\zeta_f}.
\]

The contemporaneous block signal is controlled by

\[
\theta_f
=
\frac{\lambda^2a_f^2\sigma_{qf}^2}{2\eta_f\zeta_f\psi}.
\]

For the dynamic coordination graph,

\[
\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_hZ_f)
=
2a_f^2\operatorname{Var}(X_f)\{1-\rho_f(h)\},
\]

so

\[
\theta^\Delta_f(h)
=
\frac{\lambda^2a_f^2\operatorname{Var}(X_f)\{1-\rho_f(h)\}}{\psi}.
\]

In the underdamped case,

\[
\rho_f(h)
=
e^{\zeta_f h/2}
\left[
\cos(\omega_fh)
-
\frac{\zeta_f}{2\omega_f}\sin(\omega_fh)
\right],
\qquad
\omega_f=\sqrt{-\eta_f-\frac{\zeta_f^2}{4}}.
\]

Thus the perturbation margin depends on the same quantities varied in
the simulation design: loading strength \(\lambda\), measurement-error
variance \(\psi=s_\varepsilon^2\), activity \(a_f\), shock variance
\(\sigma_{qf}^2\), position feedback \(\eta_f\), velocity feedback
\(\zeta_f\), lag \(h\), and number of indicators \(k\).

\subsection{A.6 Interpretation}\label{a.6-interpretation}

This derivation explains the target-alignment claim in the Discussion.
In the simplified population LOMM, the true dimensions are disconnected
weighted cliques or, equivalently, rank-one dynamic subspaces. Finite
samples and estimator behavior perturb that graph. Recovery is favored
when the within-block signal remains large relative to the perturbation.

DynEGA is aligned with this target because it is used here to recover
groups of variables whose changes are coordinated over time.
GraphicalVAR remains a reasonable network comparator, but the
contemporaneous partial-correlation representation conditions on other
indicators in the same reflective block and therefore compresses the
shared-source signal. GIMME is not part of this theorem because it
addresses a broader idiographic time-series problem with group-level,
subgroup-level, and individual-level paths; in this study it is best
interpreted as a flexible but computationally bounded feasibility
comparator.
