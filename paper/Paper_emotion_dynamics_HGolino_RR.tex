\begin{abstract}
Machine-learning models for facial-expression recognition can turn video
of a person into multivariate time series of classifier scores. These
scores contain measurement error from model limitations, video quality,
and observation conditions. They therefore cannot establish whether
channels labeled as positive or negative emotion reflect
lower-dimensional latent expressive processes, or whether an analysis
method can recover those processes. We introduce the Latent Oscillator
Measurement Model (LOMM), a measurement-aware simulation environment
that separately specifies oscillator-based latent dynamics, time-varying
activity, and an observation model with fallible indicators. In Study 1,
a five-fold cross-fitted exploratory comparison evaluated simulated and
real score series from 100 facial-expression video clips. Allowing clips
to differ in baseline and scale reduced the selected composite
discrepancy by 21.0\%, from 0.3250 to 0.2567. Study 2 evaluated four
methods used in network psychometrics and intensive longitudinal
analysis through a common network-community recovery target using
synthetic continuous indicators. At 100 observations per clip, with
failed or timed-out attempts counted as incorrect, overall
correct-dimension recovery was 0.939 for DynEGA, 0.884 for static EGA,
0.777 for GraphicalVAR, and 0.176 for GIMME. Independent starting states
reduced recovery for all three methods tested, indicating that
common-initial-state results were optimistic. LOMM provides a way to
test whether methods recover intended structure before empirical score
patterns are given psychological meaning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Facial-expression classifiers can turn video of a person into
multivariate time series of classifier scores. Each frame yields a
vector of scores for a chosen label set. These series have a temporal
resolution that manual coding cannot sustain.

Earlier systems usually returned scores for categories inherited from
their training data, whereas CLIP-based vision-language models can score
user-specified labels
(\citeproc{ref-foteinopoulou2023EmoCLIP}{Foteinopoulou \& Patras, 2023};
\citeproc{ref-li2023CLIPER}{Li et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-radford2021LearningTransferable}{Radford et al., 2021}).
The scores are measurements produced by a model, a prompt set, and a
video-processing pipeline. Their values also depend on image quality and
observation conditions. Benchmark performance varies with model
adaptation, label choice, and evaluation data
(\citeproc{ref-foteinopoulou2023EmoCLIP}{Foteinopoulou \& Patras, 2023};
\citeproc{ref-li2023CLIPER}{Li et al., 2023}). An interpretable pattern
among score channels may therefore reflect expressive behavior, the
measurement process, or both. The eleven-channel procedure examined here
is treated as a source of classifier scores, without a frame-level claim
that its labels measure subjective emotional experience.

This creates a psychometric problem. Several score channels may be
analyzed separately, or they may be treated as fallible indicators of
fewer latent expressive processes (\citeproc{ref-mehu2015Emotion}{Mehu
\& Scherer, 2015}; \citeproc{ref-nesselroade2002}{Nesselroade et al.,
2002}; \citeproc{ref-russellCoreAffect2003}{Russell, 2003}). Measurement
error can change the structure recovered by dynamic methods
(\citeproc{ref-kankaanpaa2026multipleindicator}{Kankaanpää et al.,
2026}). Empirical data alone cannot show whether a method would recover
the number of dimensions and the assignment of indicators to dimensions
under a known measurement structure.

We introduce the Latent Oscillator Measurement Model (LOMM) to make that
comparison possible. LOMM is a measurement-aware data generator for
multivariate intensive longitudinal data. It simulates latent processes
that change over time, specifies how strongly each process is expressed
at each observation, and converts those processes into fallible observed
indicators. The number of dimensions, indicator assignments, dynamics,
activity pattern, loadings, and measurement error are specified before a
recovery method is applied. LOMM combines oscillator models of
intraindividual dynamics with a factor-analytic observation model
(\citeproc{ref-boker2015Adaptive}{Boker, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-boker2002Method}{Boker \& Nesselroade, 2002};
\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2002}{Nesselroade et al., 2002};
\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2008dfm}{Zhang et al., 2008}).

We use LOMM in two studies. Study 1 is a five-fold cross-fitted
exploratory comparison of simulated scores with classifier scores from
100 MAFW videos. It asks whether eight descriptive features of the
simulated and real score series are similar. Study 2 evaluates four
methods used in network psychometrics and intensive longitudinal
analysis: Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis (DynEGA), static EGA,
GraphicalVAR, and GIMME. It asks whether they recover a factor structure
used to generate synthetic continuous indicators. Static EGA provides a
deliberately time-agnostic and computationally light benchmark, showing
how much structure is recoverable from contemporaneous indicator levels
alone. Study 2 also tests two consequential boundaries: whether a common
initial state makes recovery easier and whether the original indicator
grouping survives transformation to bounded scores. Because the
empirical segments are short, the Study 2 results include a \(T=25\)
panel as an ecological-length check rather than a second primary design.
Figure~\ref{fig-pipeline} summarizes the distinct questions.

\section{The Latent Oscillator Measurement
Model}\label{the-latent-oscillator-measurement-model}

LOMM represents a familiar psychometric situation: several observed
channels are fallible indicators of fewer latent processes, and those
processes change over time. Consider a simulation with three expressive
processes: approach-affiliation, threat-defense, and orienting-novelty.
Anger, fear, disgust, and anxiety could be assigned as indicators of
threat-defense. Their observed scores would share movement from that
process while retaining channel-specific measurement error. A successful
recovery method should place those indicators together and recover all
three dimensions. These labels illustrate a possible simulation design;
validation of corresponding affect categories in MAFW lies outside this
study.

LOMM generates data in three layers. The latent layer describes how each
process changes. The activity layer determines how strongly that process
is expressed at each observation. The observation layer creates multiple
indicators with prespecified loadings and measurement error. Keeping
these layers separate allows a researcher to change the dynamics,
expression pattern, or measurement quality while holding the other
components fixed.

For recording \(i\) and dimension \(f\), let \(x_{if}(t)\) denote the
latent level and \(\dot{x}_{if}(t)\) its rate of change. LOMM treats
each dimension as a linear second-order oscillator:

\begin{equation}
\ddot{x}_{if}(t)
=
\eta_f x_{if}(t)
+
\zeta_f\dot{x}_{if}(t)
+
q_{if,n},
\qquad t\in[t_n,t_{n+1}).
\label{eq:lomm-latent}
\end{equation}

The position-feedback parameter \(\eta_f\) governs the pull toward rest,
and the velocity-feedback parameter \(\zeta_f\) governs damping or
amplification. The acceleration input \(q_{if,n}\) adds irregular peaks
and reversals during the interval between observations \(n\) and
\(n+1\). Negative \(\eta_f\) and \(\zeta_f\) produce asymptotic return,
with oscillation when the characteristic roots are complex. Setting
\(\zeta_f=0\) with \(\eta_f<0\) produces undamped oscillation, while
positive \(\zeta_f\) in the tested conditions produces finite-horizon
amplification. Appendix A gives the exact transition used to advance
this system between observations, the stability conditions, and the
initialization rules.

The activity layer multiplies the latent trajectory by a nonnegative
value, \(\tilde{x}_{if,n}=a_{if,n}x_{if,n}\). A process may remain
strongly expressed, remain near a background level, or alternate with
other processes. This layer matters because an abrupt change in activity
can create a large observed change even when the underlying oscillator
has not accelerated.

The observation layer is an ordinary factor model:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf z_{in}
=
\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{in}
+
\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in},
\qquad
\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma_i).
\label{eq:lomm-measurement}
\end{equation}

Each row of \(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\) contains an indicator's loadings on
the latent dimensions, and \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}\) represents
measurement error in the observed channels. Some configurations also
allow recordings to differ in their overall baseline and scale. Study 1
estimates those components from training videos. The primary Study 2
design removes them while retaining small recording-specific differences
in loadings and residual variances. Appendix A gives the complete
observation equation and each construction.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.88\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/lomm_process_overview.pdf}

}

\caption{\label{fig-lomm-process}A worked LOMM example. The latent panel
shows the same oscillator with acceleration inputs off and on. Activity
determines how strongly the process is expressed, their product is the
expressed signal, and the observation model produces grouped indicators
that share that signal plus measurement error.}

\end{figure}%

LOMM supports two observed data forms. The primary Study 2 designs
retain the continuous indicators \(\mathbf z_{in}\), so the factor
structure used to generate the data is defined directly on the variables
being analyzed. Study 1 maps lower-dimensional coordinates to positive
scores that sum to one, matching the form of classifier output. A
targeted Study 2 condition applies the same type of bounded
transformation to the continuous indicators and tests whether their
original grouping survives. Appendix A gives the softmax, log-ratio, and
identification details.

In this paper, LOMM supplies datasets with known dimensionality and
indicator assignments. It is not fitted to the MAFW videos. Estimating
oscillator, activity, and measurement parameters from empirical data
would require additional sign, scale, temperature, and frequency
constraints, which are stated in Appendix A.

\section{Study 1: Comparing Simulated and Real Score
Series}\label{study-1-comparing-simulated-and-real-score-series}

Study 1 asks whether LOMM can reproduce selected features of real
classifier-score series. We analyzed 100 naturalistic clips from the
\emph{MAFW: A Large-scale, Multi-modal, Compound Affective Database for
Dynamic Facial Expression Recognition in the Wild} dataset
(\citeproc{ref-liu_mafw_2022}{Liu et al., 2022}). The transforEmotion
package scored up to 50 frames per clip with the \texttt{oai-large}
setting, which uses the OpenAI CLIP ViT-Large/14 model family
(\citeproc{ref-tomasevic2026TransforEmotion}{Tomašević et al., 2026}).
Eleven MAFW labels were passed directly to the text encoder. These
channels are model outputs. The dataset and scoring procedure do not
validate the labels as measures of subjective emotion.

Figure~\ref{fig-mafwvideo} shows the empirical data used as the
comparison target. The main analysis contains 3,557 observations in 188
uninterrupted clip segments from the 100 videos. Segment lengths range
from 16 to 50 observations, with a median of 35. Because these records
are short, autocorrelation and spectral summaries require caution. A
sensitivity analysis interpolated internal gaps of at most two frames.
Appendix B gives the face-processing, score-validity, missing-data, and
sampling details.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/mafw_video_dynega_example.pdf}

}

\caption{\label{fig-mafwvideo}Score series from one MAFW video clip.
Panel A shows four frames reproduced to clarify the academic analysis
under the MAFW research-use terms. Panel B shows the classifier scores
assigned to those frames. Panel C shows smoothed trajectories for the
channels with the highest mean scores. Scores were generated with
transforEmotion using OpenAI CLIP ViT-Large/14.}

\end{figure}%

Study 1 compares eight LOMM configurations formed by including or
excluding three features: clip-specific baseline, clip-specific scale,
and a brief transition in activity. The comparison uses eight summary
statistics. Six describe each channel over time: median level,
variability of levels, variability of observation-to-observation
changes, lag-1 autocorrelation, entropy of monotonic run lengths, and
normalized spectral entropy. Effective rank and total variance describe
the channels jointly. We evaluate all eight statistics on both the
bounded scores and their log-ratio coordinates.

For each statistic, discrepancy combines the standardized difference in
medians with the Wasserstein distance between the simulated and real
distributions across clips. The larger distance is retained, then the
eight statistics are averaged within each representation and the score
and log-ratio representations receive equal weight. Smaller values
indicate closer agreement.

The videos were divided into five folds. Measurement parameters and
candidate settings were estimated or selected using the training clips,
and each selected configuration generated 100 datasets matching the
held-out clips' observation indices and segment structure. The held-out
folds were used to compare the eight configurations and select the best
one, so this is a cross-fitted exploratory comparison rather than
external validation. Appendix B gives the complete discrepancy,
calibration, search, and bootstrap procedures.

\section{Study 2: Recovery of Dimensionality and Indicator
Assignments}\label{study-2-recovery-of-dimensionality-and-indicator-assignments}

Study 2 asks whether four network-based procedures recover a
prespecified number of dimensions and indicator assignments. The primary
analyses use synthetic continuous indicators. Each dataset contains
\(p=mk\) indicators, where \(m\) is the number of dimensions and \(k\)
is the number of indicators per dimension. Clips share the groups of
indicators measuring the same latent dimension and the nominal settings,
with small clip-specific loading and uniqueness jitter.
Table~\ref{tbl-scenario-words} lists the six activity-dynamics families.
Appendix B gives their exact schedules and parameters.

Every method receives the same simulated dataset. Each method estimates
its own structure, that structure is converted to an undirected network
by a prespecified rule, and the same community-detection procedure
identifies groups of indicators. The estimated grouping is then compared
with the assignment used to generate the data. This creates a common
recovery target while preserving the statistical model and row handling
of each procedure.

DynEGA is the focal method because it groups indicators whose estimated
changes are coordinated over time (\citeproc{ref-glla2010}{Boker et al.,
2010}; \citeproc{ref-golino2019investigating}{H. Golino et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{H. Golino et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-golino2017ega1}{H. F. Golino \& Epskamp, 2017}).
GraphicalVAR separates lagged relations from contemporaneous relations
and supplies the latter for grouping (\citeproc{ref-epskampggm}{Epskamp
et al., 2018}; \citeproc{ref-graphicalVAR2024}{Epskamp, 2024}). GIMME
fits each clip separately; we average residual covariance matrices from
successful fits before grouping (\citeproc{ref-gates2012group}{Gates \&
Molenaar, 2012}; \citeproc{ref-lane2019uncovering}{Lane et al., 2019}).
This use places GIMME under unusual computational strain, so its
findings concern the executed comparison and should not be generalized
to idiographic applications.

Static EGA applies EBICglasso and the same grouping step to the
contemporaneous correlation matrix, without using the observation order.
It provides a direct statistical baseline: if the prespecified groups
are already visible in contemporaneous levels, a dynamic model may not
improve this recovery target. It also provides a technical baseline
because it requires much less computation. Comparing static EGA with
DynEGA and GraphicalVAR therefore shows whether modeling temporal
structure improves grouping enough to justify its cost in these
conditions.

The primary \(T=100\) design contains 21,600 datasets. It crosses six
scenario families, two or three dimensions, 50 or 100 clips, three
loading levels, three residual-error levels, and 100 paired
replications, with four indicators per dimension. Every method receives
the same dataset within a condition and replication. Because MAFW
segments are short, with a median of 35 observations, a \(T=25\) version
of the same conditions is reported with the recovery results rather than
as a co-equal design. Louvain at resolution 1.0 is the primary grouping
algorithm; Leiden and Walktrap are paired sensitivity analyses applied
to the same fitted networks.

The headline outcome is the overall correct-dimension recovery rate,
\(P(\mathrm{valid\ output\ and\ correct\ dimension})\). It counts a
failed or timed-out attempt as an unsuccessful recovery. We also report
\(P(\mathrm{valid\ output})\) and
\(P(\mathrm{correct\ dimension}\mid\mathrm{valid\ output})\) so
computational feasibility can be separated from accuracy among valid
outputs. Assignment accuracy, adjusted Rand index, and variation of
information assess the complete indicator partition among valid outputs.
Trajectory-summary diagnostics for recovered-group summaries are
reported in Appendix C; they are not Study 2 estimands.

Three additional analyses test scope. First, paired targeted departures
alter starting states, activity transitions, cross-loadings, residual
dependence, input dependence, or the observed scale one feature at a
time. The bounded-score departure applies softmax directly to the \(p\)
continuous indicators and analyzes both the raw scores and their
centered log-ratio (CLR) components. This transformation changes the
target geometry, so the original indicator grouping need not be
preserved. Second, a restricted analysis adds clip-specific baseline and
scale differences within the loading space. Third, an extended
518,400-dataset factorial simulation evaluates DynEGA over additional
dimensions, indicators per dimension, and observation schedules.
Appendix B gives the exact method settings, departures, outcome
construction, resampling, and computing environment.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Study 1 Results: Similarity of Simulated and Real Score
Series}\label{study-1-results-similarity-of-simulated-and-real-score-series}

Figure~\ref{fig-empirical-realism} shows the score-scale discrepancy for
all eight configurations. The \emph{common baseline-and-scale
configuration} gives every clip the same baseline and scale. The
\emph{baseline-only} and \emph{baseline-and-scale} configurations allow
clips to differ in level or in both level and variability. The remaining
configurations add a brief activity transition.

\begin{figure}[H]

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/compositional_profile_discrepancy.pdf}

}

\caption{\label{fig-empirical-realism}Score-scale discrepancy between
simulated and real series for the eight configurations in the five-fold
cross-fitted exploratory comparison. Points average the eight
summary-statistic discrepancies; smaller values indicate closer
agreement. The diamond marks the baseline-and-scale configuration. The
composite used for selection weighted this score representation and the
log-ratio representation equally. Appendix C reports both
representations and the bootstrap diagnostics.}

\end{figure}%

Averaging the score and log-ratio representations, the
baseline-and-scale configuration had a discrepancy of 0.2567, compared
with 0.3250 for the common baseline-and-scale configuration. Allowing
clips to differ in baseline and scale therefore reduced the selected
composite discrepancy by 21.0\%. The baseline produced most of the
improvement, the scale helped less, and adding a brief activity
transition worsened the fit. Interpolating short internal gaps preserved
the ordering: the baseline-and-scale discrepancy was 0.2511, compared
with 0.3187 for the common configuration and 0.2707 for baseline only.

Important mismatch remained. The largest discrepancies concerned total
score variance and the variability of observation-to-observation changes
in log-ratio coordinates. The same held-out folds were used to compare
configurations and select the best one, so these findings are
exploratory. They support baseline and scale variation as a useful
starting point for simulating these score series, without validating the
classifier labels or LOMM as a model of emotional expression. Appendix C
reports every configuration, statistic, bootstrap diagnostic, and the
closest of 100 generated realizations for the example clip.

\subsection{Robustness and Sensitivity
Analyses}\label{robustness-and-sensitivity-analyses}

With \(T=25\), static EGA had the highest overall correct-dimension
recovery rate at 0.913, followed by DynEGA at 0.863 and GraphicalVAR at
0.770. Shorter records modestly reduced DynEGA recovery, left
GraphicalVAR similar, and improved static EGA. Under the specified
procedure and 900-second limit, no \(T=25\) GIMME attempt yielded a
valid network output.

Independent starting states produced the largest targeted change.
Overall correct-dimension recovery fell from 0.993 to 0.838 for DynEGA,
from 0.912 to 0.525 for static EGA, and from 0.683 to 0.253 for
GraphicalVAR. The common-initial-state design was therefore optimistic
for all three methods.

Gradual activity transitions, small cross-loadings, and correlated
inputs changed little in the tested conditions. Residual equicorrelation
of 0.10 across all indicators increased recovery for GraphicalVAR and
static EGA, but also created associations between dimensions and
therefore has no general favorable interpretation.

Bounding the scores changed the question being asked and sharply reduced
overall correct-dimension recovery on the original channels. DynEGA
recovered the correct number of dimensions in 0.333 of the CLR datasets
and 0.233 of the raw bounded-score datasets; the corresponding figures
were 0.333 and 0.167 for static EGA, and 0.087 and 0.002 for
GraphicalVAR. Direct softmax normalization and its accompanying
projection to the zero-sum subspace did not preserve the structure among
the original channels, and these results are the reason Study 1 and
Study 2 have to stay separate.

Adding clip-specific baseline and scale differences in selected stable
conditions changed DynEGA from 0.775 to 0.750, a paired difference of
\(-0.025\) with a 95\% interval of \([-0.100,0.050]\). Static EGA rose
from 0.275 to 1.000 because the added baseline lay in the loading space
and strengthened the intended grouping. GraphicalVAR and GIMME were
unchanged in this small focused analysis, but their estimates had little
support: GraphicalVAR produced only three correct outputs among 80
datasets, and every three-dimensional GIMME attempt timed out.

Median runtime among valid \(T=100\) outputs was 2.15 seconds for static
EGA, 3.69 for DynEGA, 85.41 for GraphicalVAR, and 518.16 for GIMME.
GraphicalVAR timed out on 1,271 of 21,600 attempts. GIMME produced 4,846
valid outputs, 14,477 timeouts, and 2,277 post-estimation errors. DynEGA
returned a valid output for every primary and extended-factorial
attempt. These values describe the executed workflows on one machine.
Appendix C gives the full runtime, factorial, and community-detection
results.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

LOMM addresses a common measurement problem in behavioral research. A
classifier can produce multivariate time series from video, and an
analysis can return dimensions or networks from those series, yet the
empirical data alone cannot reveal whether the intended structure was
recoverable. LOMM makes the recovery target explicit by specifying
latent dynamics, time-varying activity, and the observation model
separately. This separation is the paper's central contribution. It also
applies beyond video whenever intensive longitudinal measures are
treated as fallible indicators of lower-dimensional psychological
processes. CLIP is external to LOMM and supplies one empirical scoring
pipeline for Study 1.

Study 1 evaluated resemblance to that empirical pipeline. Allowing clips
to differ in baseline and scale reduced the selected discrepancy by
21.0\%, with most of the improvement coming from the baseline. This
result is local to 100 MAFW clips, one prompt set, one scoring model,
and the chosen summary statistics. The same held-out folds supported
comparison and selection, making the analysis exploratory. The result
supports a simulation starting point for these score series and provides
no validation of the emotion labels or of LOMM as a substantive theory
of expression.

Study 2 evaluated recovery from synthetic continuous indicators with a
prespecified factor structure. DynEGA achieved the highest overall
correct-dimension recovery and was the only method evaluated in the
extended factorial simulation. Static EGA showed that much of the
grouping was recoverable from contemporaneous levels. It exceeded
GraphicalVAR in dimensional recovery and assignment accuracy for this
target while requiring a median 2.15 seconds rather than 85.41 seconds.
This accuracy-cost result is substantively useful: temporal modeling did
not improve the stated grouping target enough to offset its added cost
in these conditions. GraphicalVAR addresses additional temporal
questions, and the comparison does not rank those broader capabilities.

The trajectory-summary diagnostics in Appendix C reinforce the
importance of defining the target. DynEGA's advantage concerned recovery
of the prespecified dimensionality and indicator partition. Alignment of
recovered-group summaries with latent trajectories produced a different
ordering. Dimensional recovery and trajectory alignment should therefore
be evaluated separately. All comparisons apply to the executed
estimation, network-conversion, and grouping procedures. GIMME's low
overall recovery mainly reflected its difficulty returning valid outputs
under an unusual clip-level fitting procedure.

Initialization placed the strongest qualification on the primary design.
A common starting state creates a shared transient across dimensions,
which adds recoverable structure outside the measurement partition.
Independent starting states reduced recovery for DynEGA, static EGA, and
GraphicalVAR, showing that the common-initial-state design was
optimistic. Gradual activity transitions, small cross-loadings, and
correlated acceleration inputs changed little in the tested conditions.
The bounded-score departure produced a sharper boundary: softmax
normalization changed associations among the original indicators, and
neither raw bounded scores nor CLR components preserved their original
grouping. The main Study 2 recovery claim therefore applies to synthetic
continuous indicators.

Several limitations define the next tests. The latent dimensions are
uncoupled. The finite-horizon amplification families produce large
late-record amplitudes and serve as stress tests. The \(T=300\)
conditions also change the observation interval, so they do not isolate
record length. The GIMME residual-covariance conversion was constructed
for this comparison and differs from typical idiographic use. Study 1
uses short clips from one controlled-access dataset and one zero-shot
scoring pipeline. LOMM is used here as a generator with prespecified
parameters; empirical estimation would require additional identification
constraints.

Three practical lessons follow. Initial conditions can create structure
unrelated to the intended measurement partition. A bounded
transformation can change the target among the analyzed variables.
Computational feasibility belongs in recovery evaluation because a
method that does not return an output cannot recover the target. Applied
work should therefore specify the measurement, latent dynamics, activity
process, observation model, and recovery target before interpreting an
empirical network. LOMM provides an environment for that sequence and
can be extended to other scoring systems, coupled latent dimensions, and
targets defined directly for bounded data.

\section{Appendix A: Complete Specification of
LOMM}\label{appendix-a-complete-specification-of-lomm}

This appendix gives the formal LOMM specification behind the conceptual
account in the main text. It is intended for readers who want to
reimplement the generator or examine its assumptions and identification
limits. Nothing in this appendix changes the reported results.

\subsection{A.1 The Latent Transition}\label{a.1-the-latent-transition}

Equation 1 gives the continuous-time oscillator. The implementation
advances its two-component state exactly over one observation interval
using

\[
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)=\exp(\mathbf{A}_f\Delta),
\qquad
\mathbf{A}_f=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
\eta_f & \zeta_f
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\text{and}
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)=\int_0^{\Delta}\exp(\mathbf{A}_f u)\mathbf{b}\,du,
\quad
\mathbf{b}=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

For the nonzero position-feedback values used in the simulation grids,
the input map has the equivalent exact form

\[
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)
=\mathbf{A}_f^{-1}\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)-\mathbf{I}_2\}\mathbf{b}.
\]

The integral definition remains valid when \(\mathbf{A}_f\) is singular,
including \(\eta_f=0\). If a root-based closed form is used numerically,
a separate repeated-root branch is required when \(\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f\)
is close to zero.

The state components \(x_{if,n}\) and \(v_{if,n}\) are the level and its
rate of change at \(t_n\); \(\eta_f\) is position feedback, \(\zeta_f\)
is velocity feedback, and \(\sigma_{qf}\) is the standard deviation of
the acceleration input. On the interval \([t_n,t_{n+1})\) the underlying
continuous-time model is
\(\ddot{x}_{if}(t)=\eta_f x_{if}(t)+\zeta_f\dot{x}_{if}(t)+q_{if,n}\)
with \(q_{if,n}\) held constant, so \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)\)
propagates the deterministic state and \(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)\)
loads the input. The inputs are independent across videos, dimensions,
and transitions, and independent of initial states, activity schedules,
and measurement errors. They are zero-order-hold acceleration inputs
rather than increments of a continuous-time white-noise diffusion. The
covariance added in one transition is
\(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)\sigma_{qf}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)^\top\).

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/lomm_three_factor_example.pdf}

}

\caption{\label{fig-latent-inputs}Illustrative latent trajectories for
three uncoupled dimensions under the exact transition, shown without and
with piecewise-constant acceleration inputs. The inputs create irregular
peaks and reversals while the feedback parameters govern the underlying

\end{figure}%

\subsection{A.2 Dynamic Regimes and Finite-Horizon
Amplification}\label{a.2-dynamic-regimes-and-finite-horizon-amplification}

The characteristic roots for dimension \(f\) are

\[
r_{f,\pm}=\frac{\zeta_f\pm\sqrt{\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f}}{2}.
\]

Continuous-time asymptotic stability requires \(\eta_f<0\) and
\(\zeta_f<0\), and the roots are oscillatory when
\(\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f<0\). The exact discrete transition is asymptotically
stable when

\[
\rho\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)\}
=\exp\!\left[\Delta\max\{\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,+}),\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,-})\}\right]<1.
\]

Negative \(\eta_f\) supplies restoring acceleration and negative
\(\zeta_f\) attenuates velocity, whereas positive \(\zeta_f\) produces
amplification. How much amplification actually appears depends on the
observed horizon. For \(T\) observations, \(H=(T-1)\Delta\). Let
\(\alpha(\mathbf{A}_f)=\max\{\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,+}),\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,-})\}\).
The quantity

\[
g_f(H)=\exp\{H\alpha(\mathbf{A}_f)\}
\]

is a modal exponential-rate factor over that horizon. It does not by
itself measure nonnormal transient amplification or the polynomial
factor that can occur at a defective repeated root. A norm-based
finite-horizon state-amplification diagnostic is

\[
G_f^{\max}(H)
=
\sup_{0\leq u\leq H}\|\exp(\mathbf{A}_fu)\|_2,
\]

and the corresponding level-only diagnostic is

\[
G_{x,f}^{\max}(H)
=
\sup_{0\leq u\leq H}
\left\|
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}
\exp(\mathbf{A}_fu)
\right\|_2.
\]

The Euclidean matrix norm is coordinate-dependent. Because the state
combines a level and a rate of change, whose components have different
units, this diagnostic is conditional on the chosen state coordinates
and should be interpreted only after fixing a nondimensionalization or
an explicit weighted state norm. A coordinate-aware definition is

\[
G_{f,M}^{\max}(H)
=
\sup_{0\leq u\leq H}
\left\|
\mathbf{M}^{1/2}\exp(\mathbf{A}_fu)\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}
\right\|_2,
\]

for a stated positive-definite state metric \(\mathbf{M}\); the
unweighted \(G_f^{\max}(H)\) corresponds to \(\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{I}_2\).
Reporting the roots and \(g_f(H)\) characterizes the modal regime over
the observed horizon, but \(g_f(H)\) should not be read as total
finite-horizon amplification. Reporting the activity schedule separately
keeps amplification in the latent dynamics distinct from nonstationarity
created by the activity layer.

\subsection{A.3 Initial States}\label{a.3-initial-states}

The system is uncoupled: \(\eta_f\), \(\zeta_f\), and \(\sigma_{qf}\)
may vary by dimension, and off-diagonal dynamic effects are omitted.
Study 2's primary simulation design initialized every dimension at
\(x_{if,1}=0.25\) and \(v_{if,1}=2\). The sensitivity analysis used
independent starting states. For a stable dimension, the stationary
covariance \(\mathbf P_f\) solves

\[
\mathbf{P}_f=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)\mathbf{P}_f\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)^\top
+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)\sigma_{qf}^2\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)^\top,
\]

whose solution is unique when \(\rho\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)\}<1\).
Drawing \(\mathbf{s}_{if,1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{P}_f)\)
makes the state process stationary before the activity layer is applied;
time-varying activity modulation still renders \(a_{if}(t)x_{if}(t)\)
nonstationary. For a dimension with positive velocity feedback, the
sensitivity draws \(x_{if,1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-0.25,0.25)\) and
\(v_{if,1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-2,2)\). These independent starts remove the
transient that all dimensions would otherwise share, while leaving the
observed horizon unchanged.

\subsection{A.4 The Activity Layer at a
Transition}\label{a.4-the-activity-layer-at-a-transition}

Away from a discontinuity in the activity value, the ordinary product
rule applies,

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\{a_{if}(t)x_{if}(t)\}
=a_{if}'(t)x_{if}(t)+a_{if}(t)x_{if}'(t).
\]

At an abrupt transition, the derivative of the activity value contains
an impulsive component in the distributional sense. Define
\(D_nx=x_{n+1}-x_n\). The exact discrete identity is

\[
D_n(a_{if}x_{if})
=
a_{if,n+1}D_nx_{if}
+
(D_na_{if})x_{if,n},
\]

where the first term uses the post-transition activity value and the
second uses the pre-transition state. The corresponding
finite-difference identity is

\[
\frac{\tilde{x}_{if,n+1}-\tilde{x}_{if,n}}{\Delta}
=a_{if,n+1}\frac{x_{if,n+1}-x_{if,n}}{\Delta}
+\frac{a_{if,n+1}-a_{if,n}}{\Delta}x_{if,n}.
\]

The second term is what a derivative-based method sees at a switch even
when the oscillator itself has not accelerated, which is the reason for
the gradual-transition sensitivity analysis. This convention attaches
the new activity value to the post-transition observation, so the
activity layer multiplies each completed latent state by the schedule
value at the same observation index.

The activity schedule takes two nonnegative values,
\(a_{\mathrm{high}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{low}}\). In non-switching
families the dominant dimension remains at \(a_{\mathrm{high}}\) for the
whole record and the others remain at \(a_{\mathrm{low}}\). In switching
families, dwell fractions determine how long each dimension remains
dominant before the schedule reassigns \(a_{\mathrm{high}}\). Appendix B
lists the numeric gains and dwell values used in the Study 2 families.

\subsection{A.5 The Observation Layer}\label{a.5-the-observation-layer}

Let \(J\) denote the number of positive score channels, let \(d=J-1\),
and let \(\mathbf V\in\mathbb R^{J\times d}\) be a fixed orthonormal
isometric log-ratio (ILR) basis satisfying
\(\mathbf V^\top\mathbf V=\mathbf I_d\) and
\(\mathbf V^\top\mathbf 1_J=\mathbf 0\). The Study 1 basis used here is
a normalized Helmert basis constructed in the stated 11-channel order.
In the full observation model, \(\boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb R^d\) is a
fixed center, \(\mathbf b_i\in\mathbb R^d\) is the clip baseline,
\(s_i>0\) is the clip scale, \(\kappa>0\) is a global scale, and
\(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\in\mathbb R^{d\times m}\) maps the \(m\)
activity-scaled latent positions into these coordinates. Conditional on
the clip-specific terms and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\), measurement errors
are independent over time and across videos, and independent of latent
states, initial states, activity schedules, and acceleration inputs,
with
\(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}\mid\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma_i)\).
Dependence across coordinates follows \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\). Designs
with fixed measurement parameters set
\(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i=\boldsymbol\Lambda\) and
\(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i=\boldsymbol\Sigma\) for every clip. The targeted
residual-equicorrelation departure sets
\(\boldsymbol\Sigma=s_\varepsilon^2\{(1-\rho)\mathbf I_p+\rho\mathbf 1_p\mathbf 1_p^\top\}\)
with \(\rho=0.10\) across all \(p\) indicators, thereby introducing
residual covariance both within and between the prespecified blocks. The
clip scale multiplies both signal and residual, whereas the baseline is
additive.

\[
\mathbf u_{in}
=
\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b_i
+\kappa s_i
\left(
\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{in}
+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}
\right).
\]

The three baseline constructions differ only in where \(\mathbf b_i\) is
allowed to live. Study 1 draws it from a zero-mean Gaussian whose
covariance is estimated from training clips in the full coordinate
space. Study 2's between-clip-variability analysis instead uses

\[
\mathbf b_i=\boldsymbol\Lambda\boldsymbol\beta_i,
\qquad E(\boldsymbol\beta_i)=\mathbf 0,
\]

so that between-clip variation stays within the space spanned by the
loadings and does not introduce structure outside the factor model.
Study 2 conditions without baseline and scale differences fix
\(\mathbf b_i=\mathbf 0\) and \(s_i=1\), giving

\[
\mathbf u_{in}
=\boldsymbol\mu
+\kappa\left(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{in}
+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}\right).
\]

In the matched-method and extended factorial designs,
\(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\) and the diagonal of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\)
receive small clip-specific perturbations to the loadings and residual
variances. The targeted sensitivity and between-clip-variability designs
use fixed equal values except where the stated condition changes them.
Thus \(\mathbf b_i=\mathbf 0\) and \(s_i=1\) remove baseline and
overall-scale differences; they do not by themselves imply a common
loading matrix or residual covariance.

The synthetic continuous indicators used in Study 2's
continuous-indicator recovery designs are the continuous-indicator
vector before any bounded-score mapping,

\[
\mathbf z_{in}=\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{in}+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in},
\]

in which each of the \(p=mk\) indicators loads on one dimension and has
diagonal measurement error.

For bounded output, LOMM maps the coordinates to the open probability
simplex. The centered log-ratio (CLR) expresses each channel relative to
the geometric mean of all channels. The isometric log-ratio (ILR)
represents the same information in \(d=J-1\) orthonormal coordinates.
Formally,

\[
\operatorname{clr}(\mathbf p)
=\log\mathbf p-
J^{-1}\{\mathbf 1_J^\top\log(\mathbf p)\}\mathbf 1_J,
\qquad
\operatorname{ilr}_{\mathbf V}(\mathbf p)
=\mathbf V^\top\operatorname{clr}(\mathbf p),
\]

with the logarithm applied componentwise. For the canonical bounded LOMM
output, applying softmax to \(\mathbf V\mathbf u_{in}/\tau\) gives

\[
\operatorname{ilr}_{\mathbf V}(\mathbf p_{in})=\frac{\mathbf u_{in}}{\tau},
\qquad
\operatorname{clr}(\mathbf p_{in})
=\frac{\mathbf V\mathbf u_{in}}{\tau},
\]

the second because \(\mathbf V\mathbf u_{in}\) lies in the zero-sum
log-ratio subspace. The fixed basis excludes the unidentified
common-logit direction and the softmax produces strictly positive rows
summing to one, so the scores identify the scaled coordinates
\(\mathbf u_{in}/\tau\) exactly. A common-logit component remains
unobservable, and the unscaled coordinates and \(\tau\) are not
separately identified without an external scale constraint.

The targeted Study 2 bounded-score departure uses the \(p=mk\)
continuous-indicator vector directly. With \(J=p\), it computes

\[
\mathbf p^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}
=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf z_{in}}{\tau}\right),
\qquad
\operatorname{clr}\!\left(\mathbf p^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}\right)
=\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbf P_p\mathbf z_{in},
\qquad
\mathbf P_p
=\mathbf I_p-\frac{\mathbf 1_p\mathbf 1_p^\top}{p}.
\]

For any orthonormal ILR basis
\(\mathbf V_p\in\mathbb R^{p\times(p-1)}\),
\(\mathbf V_p\mathbf V_p^\top=\mathbf P_p\). Defining
\(\mathbf u^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}=\mathbf V_p^\top\mathbf z_{in}\)
therefore gives
\(\mathbf V_p\mathbf u^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}=\mathbf P_p\mathbf z_{in}\).
Softmax is invariant to the removed row mean, so the direct-softmax
branch is equivalent to applying the basis form to
\(\mathbf u^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}\). The executed analysis used the \(p\)
raw scores and the \(p\) CLR components, which satisfy a zero-sum
constraint; it did not analyze the \((p-1)\) ILR coordinates. The
common-logit direction in \(\mathbf z_{in}\) is unidentifiable after
this transformation.

\subsection{A.6 Converting Period and Damping to Feedback
Parameters}\label{a.6-converting-period-and-damping-to-feedback-parameters}

Study 1's search was parameterized by natural period and damping ratio
rather than by \(\eta_f\) and \(\zeta_f\) directly. If \(P_f\) is the
natural period in observation intervals and \(\xi_f\) is the damping
ratio, then

\[
\omega_{n,f}=\frac{2\pi}{P_f\Delta},
\qquad
\eta_f=-\omega_{n,f}^2,
\qquad
\zeta_f=-2\xi_f\omega_{n,f},
\]

and the damped angular frequency is
\(\omega_{d,f}=\omega_{n,f}\sqrt{1-\xi_f^2}\). With \(P_f=12\),
\(\xi_f=0.5\), and \(\Delta=1\), these give \(\eta_f=-0.2742\) and
\(\zeta_f=-0.5236\); scaling the inputs to unit stationary position
variance gives \(\sigma_{qf}=0.5418\).

\subsection{A.7 Identifiability}\label{a.7-identifiability}

For any nonsingular diagonal matrix \(\mathbf{D}\),

\[
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{in}
=(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{D}^{-1})(\mathbf{D}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{in}),
\]

so loading magnitude and latent-trajectory scale are not jointly
identified without an explicit scale constraint. Activity gain and
acceleration-input scale contribute to latent amplitude in the same way.
The softmax layer adds invariance to a common location shift and a
tradeoff between the temperature and the coordinate scale. Dimension
labels are recoverable only up to permutation.

Sampling cadence adds an aliasing restriction. For an underdamped
dimension, write

\[
r_{f,\pm}=\frac{\zeta_f}{2}\pm i\omega_f,
\qquad
\omega_f=
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-(\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f)}.
\]

At observation times \(t_n=(n-1)\Delta\), the frequencies \(\omega_f\)
and \(\omega_f+2\pi q/\Delta\) for integer \(q\) generate the same
sampled sine and cosine sequences after an appropriate change in initial
velocity. Identifying continuous-time oscillator parameters from data
therefore requires a cadence-dependent restriction such as

\[
0<\omega_f<\frac{\pi}{\Delta},
\]

together with a specified acceleration-input model and constraints on
the latent state basis. Estimating LOMM from data would in total require
sign and scale constraints on the dimensions, normalization of the
activity schedule, anchoring of either the loading scale or the input
scale, a reference-logit or sum-to-zero constraint, a fixed or otherwise
identified temperature, and this frequency restriction. Every
oscillatory mode used here satisfies the restriction: Study 1 uses
\(\Delta=1\), giving the most restrictive bound \(\pi\approx3.14\)
against a largest angular frequency of approximately 0.78, and Study 2
uses \(\Delta\in\{0.25,0.4\}\), giving bounds of approximately 12.57 and
7.85 against a largest angular frequency of approximately 1.04. The
simulations do not estimate any of these parameters.

\subsection{B.1 Study 1 Scoring and
Calibration}\label{b.1-study-1-scoring-and-calibration}

The Study 1 sample was assembled one clip at a time, with MAFW category
labels used to diversify the sample rather than as validation targets.
The main analysis contains 3,557 observations in 188 uninterrupted
segments from 100 clips. Segment lengths range from 16 to 50
observations, with a median of 35. The gap-interpolated sensitivity
analysis contains 3,632 observations in 129 segments, with lengths from
17 to 50 and a median of 36.5.

transforEmotion sampled up to 50 frames uniformly from each video and
used the OpenAI CLIP ViT-Large/14 model family. A Haar cascade detected
faces, and the largest detected face was expanded by 50 pixels on each
side before classification. Frames without a detected face received
missing scores. Eleven lowercase class labels were passed directly to
the text encoder without a sentence template: anger, disgust, fear,
happiness, neutral, sadness, surprise, contempt, anxiety, helplessness,
and disappointment. Burned-in subtitles were not masked and could
contribute when they fell inside the padded face crop.

Scores were required to lie in \([0,1]\). A complete row was retained
when its sum was positive, finite, and within \(10^{-6}\) of one, then
divided by that sum in the fixed channel order. Values were not
truncated and zero replacement was disabled. The main analysis retained
complete rows. The sensitivity analysis interpolated internal gaps of at
most two frames within a segment. The scoring-time package release and
exact checkpoint revision were not archived, so exact frame rescoring
cannot be guaranteed; the retained scores support the reported analyses.

The comparison used eight statistics. Median level, interquartile range
of levels, interquartile range of observation-to-observation changes,
lag-1 autocorrelation, entropy of monotonic run lengths, and normalized
spectral entropy were calculated for each channel. Effective rank and
total variance described all channels jointly. Each statistic was
evaluated on both bounded scores and log-ratio coordinates.

For every statistic and representation, disagreement between simulated
and real clip distributions was measured by the absolute difference
between medians and the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance. Both were
standardized by the spread of the real values, using the interquartile
range when available, followed by \(1.4826\) times the median absolute
deviation, the standard deviation, or one. The larger standardized
distance was retained. The composite averaged the eight statistics
within each representation and weighted the two representations equally.

Clips were divided into five deterministic folds within MAFW category
and length order, leaving 20 test clips per fold. The coordinate center,
loading directions, loading scales, and covariance of clip baselines
were estimated from training clips only. The first three eigenvectors of
the within-clip covariance supplied the loading directions. Their
orientation and scale were fixed deterministically. The baseline
covariance was shrunk halfway toward its diagonal and eigenvalues were
floored at \(10^{-8}\).

Within each fold and configuration, the search crossed natural periods
\(\{8,12,24\}\), damping ratios \(\{0.05,0.20,0.50\}\), and observation
scales \(\kappa\in\{0.75,1.00,1.25\}\). Three paired training datasets
evaluated each of the 27 candidates. Selection minimized the equally
weighted representation-level discrepancy, with deterministic
tie-breaking. All folds selected a 12-observation period, damping ratio
0.50, and \(\kappa=1\) for the baseline-and-scale configuration.

Each selected configuration generated 100 datasets per test fold,
matching the held-out observation indices and segment structure. The
comparison concerns distributions of summary statistics across clips,
rather than frame-level reconstruction. Uncertainty used 2,000 paired
bootstrap resamples of the 20 clips within each fold. The eight
configurations are the factorial combinations of clip baseline, clip
scale, and brief activity transition.

\subsection{B.3 Method
Implementations}\label{b.3-method-implementations}

DynEGA estimates first derivatives from time-delay embeddings,
constructs an EBICglasso partial-correlation network, and groups its
positive edges (\citeproc{ref-glla2010}{Boker et al., 2010};
\citeproc{ref-golino2019investigating}{H. Golino et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{H. Golino et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-golino2017ega1}{H. F. Golino \& Epskamp, 2017}). Video
identity prevents embedding windows from crossing clip boundaries. An
explicit search selects the embedding dimension by the Total Entropy Fit
Index, with shorter embeddings breaking ties. Candidate dimensions are
\(\{5,10,15,25,50\}\) for \(T=100\) and \(\{5,6,10,12\}\) for \(T=25\).
EGAnet 2.3.0 uses tau 1, delta 1, first derivatives, automatic
correlations, pairwise missing-data handling, and the glasso model. The
derivative scale does not affect the correlation matrix because
conversion from observation units to physical time multiplies every
derivative by the same positive constant.

Static EGA applies EBICglasso to the contemporaneous correlation matrix
of the stacked indicator levels and then uses the same
network-processing and community-detection rules as DynEGA. It ignores
the observation order. This makes it a time-agnostic benchmark for the
grouping target and a computational benchmark for the temporal
procedures.

GraphicalVAR uses mlGraphicalVAR with clip identity as the grouping
variable, within-clip centering, variable standardization, and gamma 0.5
(\citeproc{ref-epskampggm}{Epskamp et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-graphicalVAR2024}{Epskamp, 2024}). Lagged pairs never
cross clip boundaries. The grouping uses the returned fixedPCC
contemporaneous residual partial-correlation matrix. The diagonal is
removed and the matrix is treated as an undirected weighted graph.

GIMME fits each standardized clip separately with unified structural
equation modeling and vector autoregression
(\citeproc{ref-gates2012group}{Gates \& Molenaar, 2012};
\citeproc{ref-lane2019uncovering}{Lane et al., 2019}). Standardized
residual covariance matrices from successful fits are aligned by
variable name, averaged across clips, and symmetrized. The resulting
graph is a residual-covariance graph. Unequal residual variances can
therefore influence its weights. This conversion was built for the
present comparison and differs from typical idiographic use.

For every method, negative graph weights are set to zero. If no positive
edge remains, absolute values of the original off-diagonal weights are
used; only an all-zero matrix produces singleton communities. The
integrated outputs do not record the frequency of this fallback. Louvain
at resolution 1.0 is primary (\citeproc{ref-blondel2008fast}{Blondel et
al., 2008}). Leiden and Walktrap are applied to the same fitted network
as sensitivity analyses (\citeproc{ref-pons2006walktrap}{Pons \& Latapy,
2005}; \citeproc{ref-traag2019leiden}{Traag et al., 2019}). A
deterministic offset from the dataset seed initializes each method fit.

GraphicalVAR and GIMME were omitted from the 518,400-dataset extended
factorial simulation because the full design was computationally
impractical. Both were included in the primary and shorter-series
designs. GraphicalVAR was also included in the targeted and between-clip
analyses. GIMME was included in the between-clip analysis and excluded
from the targeted one-at-a-time departures.

\subsection{B.4 Outcomes, Uncertainty, and
Computing}\label{b.4-outcomes-uncertainty-and-computing}

The valid-output rate records whether a procedure returned a network
that could be grouped. The conditional correct-dimension rate records
correct dimensionality among valid outputs. The headline overall
correct-dimension recovery rate requires both events, so errors and
timeouts remain in its denominator.

Adjusted Rand index and variation of information compare the full
estimated and prespecified partitions among valid outputs. Assignment
accuracy matches estimated groups to dimensions to maximize correct
indicator assignments. Every indicator remains in the denominator, and
indicators in unmatched groups count as incorrect.

Trajectory-summary diagnostics are constructed after grouping.
Indicators are standardized and each estimated group is summarized by
its first principal component, with a row mean used when a component
cannot be computed. Correlation pairs each prespecified dimension with
the estimated summary having the largest absolute correlation. NRMSE
uses one-to-one matching after sign reversal when needed. An unmatched
data-generating dimension receives the penalty 2. All-attempt
failure-adjusted versions assign correlation 0 and NRMSE 2 when no valid
network output exists.

Uncertainty uses 2,000 bootstrap resamples of whole datasets, stratified
by condition. Monte Carlo standard errors use delete-one-dataset
jackknifing. The three community-detection algorithms reuse the same
fitted structure and therefore assess the grouping step rather than
estimator variability.

Analyses used R 4.5.1 on Rocky Linux 8.8 with dual Intel Xeon Silver
4310 processors, 48 logical threads, and 125 GiB RAM. Key versions were
EGAnet 2.3.0, graphicalVAR 0.3.4, gimme 10.0, igraph 2.2.1, qgraph
1.9.8, psych 2.5.6, lavaan 0.6-20, dplyr 1.1.4, and ggplot2 4.0.1.
GraphicalVAR and GIMME used a 900-second limit per fit in the primary
and shorter designs.

Runtime is elapsed wall-clock time for attempts returning a valid
network output. It includes structure estimation, network extraction,
and community detection. One fitted structure is reused for Louvain,
Leiden, and Walktrap, so feasibility counts unique fits and accuracy
counts fit-and-algorithm results. Runtime values are specific to this
implementation and machine.
