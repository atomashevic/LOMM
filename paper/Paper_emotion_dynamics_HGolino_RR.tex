\title{A Latent Oscillator Measurement Model to Simulate
Emotional-Expression Score Dynamics in Video}
\author{Aleksandar Tomašević \and Hudson Golino \and Alexander P.
Christensen}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
Automated vision models turn video into dense time series of
classifier-output scores for visible expression. Dynamic analyses of
those scores need a measurement check: a recovery method should find the
latent organization that generated the score channels after accounting
for the observation process. We introduce the Latent Oscillator
Measurement Model (LOMM), a simulation framework that generates latent
oscillator trajectories, gates their activity over time, and maps them
through an explicit observation model into noisy score channels. The
default observation profile works in identified log-ratio contrast
coordinates, includes clip-specific baselines and positive clip scale,
and maps the contrasts to the probability simplex. Empirical,
loading-aligned, and zero-baseline geometries are explicit options, and
the homogeneous profile is exactly nested. We calibrated selected
temporal and dimensional score-series targets against MAFW-derived
classifier-output panels. The completed broad recovery benchmark used a
separately versioned historical homogeneous linear generator. In a broad
DynEGA grid and a matched comparison with GraphicalVAR and GIMME, DynEGA
gave the most consistent dimensional and block recovery in these LOMM
simulation designs and completed the broad grid. GraphicalVAR often
recovered dimensionality but was slower and more sensitive to weak
measurement conditions. GIMME had substantial runtime and completion
limits in this design. LOMM gives method researchers a controlled
setting for testing whether dynamic recovery methods can recover latent
score organization before classifier-output score dynamics are
interpreted substantively.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Video is increasingly used as a source of intensive behavioral data
because it preserves the temporal ordering of visible action and can be
analyzed at a resolution that manual frame-by-frame coding cannot
sustain. Deep-learning systems can now convert frames or short video
segments into dense time series of model-derived emotional-expression
scores (\citeproc{ref-li2022Deep}{S. Li \& Deng, 2022};
\citeproc{ref-meng2017IdentityAware}{Meng et al., 2017}). In a
facial-expression recognition task, a model maps each image or segment
to category probabilities or continuous scores for labels such as
happiness, anger, sadness, fear, surprise, disgust, or neutral
expression.

These score streams are valuable because many behavioral questions
concern how visible affect unfolds over time. Researchers may ask
whether expressive states persist or switch, whether they return after
perturbation, and whether many model-output channels reflect a smaller
number of expressive dimensions. Such questions arise in psychotherapy,
autism assessment, collaborative work, public communication, and
social-media video
(\citeproc{ref-halamovaAutomatedFacialExpression2023}{Halamová et al.,
2023}; \citeproc{ref-holidayMultimodalEmotionPerspective2023}{Holiday et
al., 2023}; \citeproc{ref-liDecodingGroupEmotional2024}{Q. Li et al.,
2024};
\citeproc{ref-manelisbaramComparingThreeAlgorithms2025}{Manelis-Baram et
al., 2025}).

Automated facial-analysis systems now make this kind of work feasible at
scale. Datasets such as FER2013 supported deep learning approaches to
facial-expression recognition, especially Convolutional Neural Networks
(\citeproc{ref-arriaga2017Realtimea}{Arriaga et al., 2017};
\citeproc{ref-ezerceli2022Convolutional}{Ezerceli \& Eskil, 2022}; CNNs;
\citeproc{ref-goodfellow2013Challenges}{Goodfellow et al., 2013}). Most
early systems inherited their emotion categories from the training data
and focused on fixed classification tasks, commonly returning
probabilities for seven categories: anger, disgust, fear, happiness,
sadness, surprise, and neutral facial expression
(\citeproc{ref-wang2022Rethinking}{Wang et al., 2022}). More recently,
CLIP-based and related vision-language models have enabled zero-shot
classification with user-specified emotion labels
(\citeproc{ref-foteinopoulou2023EmoCLIP}{Foteinopoulou \& Patras, 2023};
\citeproc{ref-li2023CLIPER}{H. Li et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-radford2021LearningTransferable}{Radford et al., 2021};
\citeproc{ref-tomasevic2026TransforEmotion}{Tomašević et al., 2026}),
making it possible to represent each video frame as a vector of
prompt-defined classifier scores.

The main obstacle is measurement. Emotional-expression scores are
trained-model outputs that index visible expressive behavior through a
scoring procedure. Facial-expression technologies can be interpreted as
tools for emotion recognition or social-interaction analysis, and those
interpretations answer different questions
(\citeproc{ref-mckeownAreFacialExpression2024}{Allison \& McKeown,
2023}). A separate psychometric question is whether multiple score
channels should be treated as variables in their own right or as
fallible indicators of lower-dimensional expressive processes
(\citeproc{ref-mehu2015Emotion}{Mehu \& Scherer, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2013dynamic}{Nesselroade et al., 2013};
\citeproc{ref-russellCoreAffect2003}{Russell, 2003}). Measurement error
can alter the structure recovered by dynamic methods, so method
validation must separate latent temporal organization from the
observation process used to measure it
(\citeproc{ref-kankaanpaa2026multipleindicator}{Kankaanpää et al.,
2026}).

The remaining methodological gap is practical. Researchers need a
generator in which latent expressive dynamics and the multichannel
observation process are known before any recovery method is applied.
Oscillator models describe level, rate of change, damping, and
perturbation in intraindividual dynamics
(\citeproc{ref-boker2015Adaptive}{Boker, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-boker2002Method}{Boker \& Nesselroade, 2002};
\citeproc{ref-ollero2023Characterizing}{Ollero et al., 2025}). A useful
validation design must vary dimensionality, indicator blocks,
measurement quality, and dynamic regime while keeping the recovery
target known.

This paper introduces the Latent Oscillator Measurement Model (LOMM), a
generative simulation model for model-derived emotional-expression score
channels. LOMM first generates latent expressive trajectories from
second-order oscillator dynamics and then maps those latent processes
into classifier-output score channels through an explicit measurement
layer. The model lets researchers control latent dimensionality,
feedback regime, loading strength, measurement error, and activity
pattern before testing whether dynamic methods recover the generating
structure.

LOMM integrates several established modeling ideas in one inspectable
generator. Latent differential-equation models represent level and
velocity, continuous-time state-space models motivate the exact
transition between observation times, dynamic factor models motivate a
multivariate process representation, and the measurement layer separates
latent trajectories from fallible observed indicators
(\citeproc{ref-boker2015Adaptive}{Boker, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-boker2002Method}{Boker \& Nesselroade, 2002};
\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2002}{Nesselroade et al., 2002};
\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2008dfm}{Zhang et al., 2008}). The
network-recovery task then supplies a known indicator partition against
which fitted structures can be evaluated. The contribution is this
explicit bridge from latent dynamics through classifier-output
measurement to structural recovery; the individual oscillator,
state-space, factor-model, and graph-estimation components retain their
established interpretations.

\subsection{The Latent Oscillator Measurement
Model}\label{the-latent-oscillator-measurement-model}

LOMM starts from a measurement object: a video produces frame-level
classifier-output score channels for visible expression. The simulation
treats those channels as fallible indicators of lower-dimensional
expressive processes. A short clip can contain low expressivity, brief
expressive episodes, gradual recovery, persistent activation, or
alternation between expressive regimes. The latent representation
therefore needs to describe level, rate of change, return toward
equilibrium, overshoot, damping, and perturbation, concepts that are
central to affective-dynamics research
(\citeproc{ref-hollensteinStateSpaceGrids2007}{Hollenstein, 2007};
\citeproc{ref-houbenRelationShorttermEmotion2015}{Houben et al., 2015};
\citeproc{ref-kuppensEmotionDynamics2017}{Kuppens \& Verduyn, 2017}).

Oscillator models provide a compact language for those features. Prior
work has used second-order oscillator representations to describe
intraindividual dynamics in which a current level and its rate of change
jointly determine subsequent movement
(\citeproc{ref-boker2015Adaptive}{Boker, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-boker2002Method}{Boker \& Nesselroade, 2002};
\citeproc{ref-ollero2023Characterizing}{Ollero et al., 2025}). LOMM uses
the same state representation for multivariate simulation and
measurement evaluation. It combines three layers: a latent oscillator
layer that generates expressive trajectories, an activity layer that
controls which latent dimension is expressed over time, and an
observation layer that supports compositional classifier-output panels
and explicitly versioned block-recovery surfaces
(\citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{H. Golino et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2013dynamic}{Nesselroade et al., 2013}).

The latent layer treats each expressive dimension as a second-order
state process advanced in discrete simulation steps. For state
\(\mathbf{s}_{if,t}=[x_{if,t},v_{if,t}]^\top\), the implemented exact
linear step is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_{if,t+dt}
=
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(dt)\mathbf{s}_{if,t}
+
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(dt)q_{if,t},
\qquad
q_{if,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{qf}^{2}),
\label{eq:lomm-latent}
\end{equation}

where

\[
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(dt)=\exp(\mathbf{A}_f dt),
\qquad
\mathbf{A}_f=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
\eta_f & \zeta_f
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\text{and}
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(dt)=\int_0^{dt}\exp(\mathbf{A}_f u)\mathbf{b}\,du,
\quad
\mathbf{b}=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

For the nonzero position-feedback values used in the simulation grids,
the input map has the equivalent exact form

\[
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(dt)
=\mathbf{A}_f^{-1}\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(dt)-\mathbf{I}_2\}\mathbf{b}.
\]

The integral definition remains valid at singular parameter values and
supplies the continuous limit used by the implementation near repeated
roots.

In Equation \ref{eq:lomm-latent}, \(i\) indexes videos, \(f\) indexes
latent expressive dimensions, and \(t\) indexes discrete simulation time
points spaced \(dt\) apart. The state variable \(x_{if,t}\) is the
latent level of dimension \(f\) for video \(i\), and \(v_{if,t}\) is its
rate of change. The parameter \(\eta_f\) is position feedback,
\(\zeta_f\) is velocity feedback, \(q_{if,t}\) is the stepwise Gaussian
acceleration input used at each transition, and \(\sigma_{qf}\) is the
standard deviation of that input. Within a step, the recursion is the
exact transition of the linear second-order oscillator
\(\ddot{x}_{if}=\eta_f x_{if}+\zeta_f \dot{x}_{if}+q_{if,t}\) with the
shock held constant, so \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(dt)\) propagates the
deterministic state and \(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(dt)\) is the
zero-order-hold loading through which \(q_{if,t}\) enters as a per-step
acceleration input.

The dynamic parameters set the latent regime. The characteristic roots
for factor \(f\) are

\[
r_{f,\pm}=\frac{\zeta_f\pm\sqrt{\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f}}{2}.
\]

Continuous-time asymptotic stability requires \(\eta_f<0\) and
\(\zeta_f<0\), and the roots are oscillatory when
\(\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f<0\). The exact discrete transition is asymptotically
stable when

\[
\rho\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(dt)\}
=\exp\!\left[dt\max\{\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,+}),\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,-})\}\right]<1.
\]

Negative \(\eta_f\) supplies restoring acceleration, negative
\(\zeta_f\) attenuates velocity, and positive \(\zeta_f\) produces
anti-damped amplification over a finite interval. For a sequence with
\(T\) observations, the observed horizon is \(H=(T-1)dt\), and the
corresponding deterministic growth diagnostic is
\(g_f(H)=\exp\{H\max\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,+},r_{f,-})\}\). Reporting
\(T\), \(dt\), \(H\), the roots, and \(g_f(H)\) distinguishes stable
oscillation, gated nonstationarity, and finite-horizon amplification.

The model uses an uncoupled multivariate system: \(\eta_f\),
\(\zeta_f\), and \(\sigma_{qf}\) may vary by factor, and off-diagonal
dynamic effects are omitted. The checked-in simulations initialized
every factor at \(x_{if,1}=0.25\) and \(v_{if,1}=2\). The revised
robustness protocol uses independent starting states. For a stable
factor, it solves

\[
\mathbf{P}_f=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f\mathbf{P}_f\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f^\top
+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f\sigma_{qf}^2\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f^\top
\]

and draws \(\mathbf{s}_{if,1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{P}_f)\).
For an anti-damped factor, it draws
\(x_{if,1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-0.25,0.25)\) and
\(v_{if,1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-2,2)\). These independent starts remove the
common deterministic transient from the revised sensitivity design while
leaving the observed horizon unchanged.

The activity layer turns raw oscillator states into scenario-specific
expression episodes. The trajectory passed to the measurement model is
\(\tilde{x}_{if,t}=a_{if,t}x_{if,t}\), where \(a_{if,t}\geq 0\) is a
scenario-defined activity envelope generated from active gains,
background gains, and dwell schedules. Its derivative contains both
oscillator motion and gate motion,

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\{a_{if}(t)x_{if}(t)\}
=a_{if}'(t)x_{if}(t)+a_{if}(t)x_{if}'(t).
\]

For the piecewise-constant schedules used in the checked-in simulations,
the corresponding finite-difference identity is

\[
\frac{\tilde{x}_{if,t+dt}-\tilde{x}_{if,t}}{dt}
=a_{if,t+dt}\frac{x_{if,t+dt}-x_{if,t}}{dt}
+\frac{a_{if,t+dt}-a_{if,t}}{dt}x_{if,t}.
\]

In single-regime scenarios, one dimension has higher activity across the
full sequence; in switching scenarios, the active dimension changes
after sampled dwell intervals; and in low-expression scenarios, all
dimensions remain close to background activity. Abrupt gates can
introduce a derivative signal at a switch even when oscillator
acceleration is unchanged. The revised challenge design therefore
compares the checked-in step gates with five-observation half-cosine
transitions while preserving the generating dimensions and indicator
memberships.

The observation layer maps the activity-scaled latent trajectories into
an identified contrast space before placing them on their reported
scale. Let \(J\) denote the number of positive classifier-output
channels, let \(d=J-1\), and let \(\mathbf V\in\mathbb R^{J\times d}\)
be a fixed orthonormal ILR basis satisfying
\(\mathbf V^\top\mathbf V=\mathbf I_d\) and
\(\mathbf V^\top\mathbf 1_J=\mathbf 0\). For video \(i\) and frame
\(t\), the pre-temperature contrast vector is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf u_{it}
=\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b_i
+\kappa s_i\left(\boldsymbol\Lambda\tilde{\mathbf x}_{it}
+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{it}\right),
\qquad
\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{it}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma_i).
\label{eq:lomm-measurement}
\end{equation}

In Equation \ref{eq:lomm-measurement}, \(\boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb R^d\)
is a fixed contrast-space center, \(\mathbf b_i\in\mathbb R^d\) is a
clip baseline, \(s_i>0\) is a clip-specific profile scale, \(\kappa>0\)
is a global observation scale,
\(\boldsymbol\Lambda\in\mathbb R^{d\times m}\) maps the \(m\)
activity-scaled latent positions into contrast coordinates, and
\(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{it}\) is contrast-space measurement error. The
placement of \(s_i\) is part of the model: it multiplies both the
dynamic signal and the measurement residual, while \(\mathbf b_i\)
remains additive.

The public LOMM pipeline uses the \texttt{compositional\_bs} profile by
default. Its empirical baseline mode draws \(\mathbf b_i\) from a
zero-mean Gaussian distribution with a training-derived between-clip
covariance in the locked contrast coordinates. Its clip scale follows
\(s_i\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0.5,1.5)\), which is positive and has population
mean one. The calibrated preset fixes a 12-frame oscillator period,
damping ratio 0.5, \(\kappa=1\), \(\tau=1\), and contrast-residual
standard deviation 0.25. The additional smooth-gate component evaluated
in the observation-profile ablation is disabled in this preset.

The baseline geometry is an explicit simulation choice. The
\texttt{empirical} mode uses the training-derived covariance in the full
active contrast space. The \texttt{loading\_aligned} mode uses

\[
\mathbf b_i=\boldsymbol\Lambda\boldsymbol\beta_i,
\qquad E(\boldsymbol\beta_i)=\mathbf 0,
\]

so between-clip variation remains in the declared loading space for
known-truth recovery experiments. The \texttt{none} mode fixes
\(\mathbf b_i=\mathbf 0\). Clip scale is controlled separately; the
homogeneous profile combines \texttt{none} with \(s_i=1\) and is
therefore an exact nested case:

\[
\mathbf u_{it}
=\boldsymbol\mu
+\kappa\left(\boldsymbol\Lambda\tilde{\mathbf x}_{it}
+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{it}\right).
\]

For compositional output, LOMM maps the identified contrasts to the open
probability simplex:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf p_{it}
=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf V\mathbf u_{it}}{\tau}\right),
\qquad
\operatorname{ilr}_{\mathbf V}(\mathbf p_{it})=\frac{\mathbf u_{it}}{\tau}.
\label{eq:lomm-composition}
\end{equation}

The fixed ILR basis excludes the unidentified common-logit direction,
and the softmax produces strictly positive rows that sum to one. A
caller can retain \(\mathbf u_{it}\) directly as a linear contrast
surface when the estimand requires an unbounded known-truth panel. The
completed revision-v2 method comparison used the historical homogeneous
linear form
\(\mathbf z_{it}=\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{it}+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{it}\).
In that benchmark, each of the \(p=mk\) observed indicators belongs to
one block \(B_f\), has one primary loading with item-level jitter, and
has diagonal measurement error with item-level variance jitter. The
loading-aligned B+S bridge also used the pre-link \texttt{linear\_z}
representation. It screens the effect of B+S on selected known-block
recovery conditions and does not validate recovery from bounded
probabilities or CLR panels.

These observed channels are classifier-output measurements of visible
expression. The model tests whether recovery methods recover a
prespecified observation process under controlled loading strength,
measurement error, dynamic regime, activity regime, and baseline
geometry. Semantic label validity and subjective emotional experience
remain outside the evidential scope of the simulation. Neutral can
appear as an empirical classifier-output channel, but it is not assigned
a source-truth latent factor unless a neutral block is specified by
design.

As an illustrative substantive mapping, a three-factor LOMM could
represent approach-affiliation, threat-defense, and orienting-novelty
dimensions. Prompt-defined classifier-output channels related to
happiness, amusement, trust, or smiling could form an
approach-affiliation block. Channels related to anger, fear, disgust,
contempt, or anxiety could form a threat-defense block. Channels related
to surprise, anticipation, confusion, or attention could form an
orienting-novelty block. These labels are examples for simulation
design, not validated affect categories in the empirical MAFW panels.
The example draws on affect taxonomies that distinguish broad
dimensions, discrete expression families, and opponent tendencies
(\citeproc{ref-plutchikNatureEmotions2001}{Plutchik, 2001};
\citeproc{ref-russellCircumplexModel1980}{Russell, 1980},
\citeproc{ref-russellCoreAffect2003}{2003};
\citeproc{ref-russellMehrabianThreeFactor1977}{Russell \& Mehrabian,
1977}), and it is consistent with facial-expression benchmarks that
combine categorical expression labels with dimensional ratings
(\citeproc{ref-kolliasABAW2023}{Kollias et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-mehu2015Emotion}{Mehu \& Scherer, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-mollahosseiniAffectNetDatabase2019}{Mollahosseini et al.,
2019}). Alternative affective taxonomies can be encoded by changing the
latent block map while keeping the LOMM architecture fixed.

\subsubsection{Model Scope and
Identifiability}\label{model-scope-and-identifiability}

The known simulation settings do not imply that every LOMM component can
be identified from an empirical score panel. Block labels are
recoverable only up to permutation, and latent trajectory signs and
scales depend on the chosen constraints. For any nonsingular diagonal
matrix \(\mathbf{D}\),

\[
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{it}
=(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{D}^{-1})(\mathbf{D}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{it}),
\]

so loading magnitude and latent-trajectory scale are not jointly
identified without scale constraints. Activity gain and innovation scale
also contribute to latent amplitude. The softmax layer adds
common-location invariance and a temperature--scale tradeoff. LOMM is
used here as a controlled generator with known settings and a
prespecified partition target. Empirical use of the framework would
require additional scale, loading, and observation-model constraints
before its dynamic parameters could be interpreted as estimates.

\subsection{Present Research}\label{present-research}

Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis {[}DynEGA; H. Golino et al.
(\citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{2022}){]} is the focal
dynamic-dimensionality estimator because it was developed to recover
groups of variables whose estimated changes are coordinated over time.
This role supplies a substantive reason to include DynEGA in the design
before its performance is known. GraphicalVAR and GIMME provide adjacent
intensive-longitudinal pipelines with different estimands and
graph-conversion steps, while static EGA evaluates whether temporal
preprocessing adds recovery information beyond contemporaneous score
levels. A true-block oracle checks the simulation and partition code
against the known measurement structure.

The revised study addresses three questions. First, can LOMM reproduce
held-out observable properties of empirical video-derived
classifier-output scores without allowing clip boundaries or
training-derived summaries to enter the evaluation fold? Second, how do
DynEGA, GraphicalVAR, GIMME, and static EGA recover the same known block
structure on matched panels, and where does the oracle place the
implementation ceiling? Third, how does recovery change under short
records, alternative initialization and stability regimes, smooth
activity transitions, cross-loadings, correlated errors and shocks,
bounded score representations, and estimator feasibility limits?

The evaluation begins with five-fold clip-level empirical calibration
and a matched method comparison on identical generated panels. The
matched design provides the primary evidence because panel keys,
simulation seeds, structural targets, and the downstream partition rule
are shared across pipelines. A broad DynEGA grid then describes the
focal method's wider operating envelope. A separate \(T=25\)
same-cadence study and a bounded challenge suite evaluate departures
that are especially relevant to short classifier-output streams and
nonideal measurement. These revision-v2 analyses retain their original
profile identifiers, generator hashes, panel keys, and output
namespaces. The later B/S/G ablation and recovery bridge are separate,
versioned evidence for the current public observation default.

Appendix A gives the population argument that motivates the recovery
predictions. Under ideal block measurement, common within-block signal
and independent cross-block processes produce separated population
components. Weaker loadings, larger measurement error, lower activity,
and cross-block dependence reduce the weakest within-block recovery
margin. The simulations test these predictions as panel-paired changes
while separating usable-output probability from recovery conditional on
a usable output.

\section{Method}\label{method}

The analyses follow the workflow in Figure~\ref{fig-pipeline}. We first
use empirical video-score panels to define selected realism targets for
model-derived expression-score series (Panels A--C). We then translate
those targets into LOMM scenario conditions (Panel D). The public
observation pipeline applies the empirical B+S compositional profile by
default, with loading-aligned B+S and homogeneous linear profiles as
explicit alternatives (Panels E--G). The historical revision-v2
generator and the \texttt{linear\_z} bridge occupy separately versioned
recovery paths. Recovery methods are evaluated only on panel surfaces
whose dimensionality, indicator blocks, baseline geometry, and reported
scale are declared by the simulation design (Panel H).

\subsection{Empirical Calibration and Realism
Targets}\label{empirical-calibration-and-realism-targets}

Figure~\ref{fig-mafwvideo} illustrates the empirical score-series
surface used to motivate the simulation study. MAFW, introduced as
\emph{MAFW: A Large-scale, Multi-modal, Compound Affective Database for
Dynamic Facial Expression Recognition in the Wild}, supplies
naturalistic video clips for calibrating selected score-series
statistics (\citeproc{ref-liu_mafw_2022}{Liu et al., 2022}). The dataset
does not validate the classifier-output labels, the scoring model, or
the subjective emotional states of the people in the clips. The four
frames identify the dataset-derived scoring example. The repository and
archive do not include raw MAFW videos, and access to the original video
materials remains governed by the dataset providers. The realism check
uses a 100-clip calibration sample scored with the same zero-shot
procedure as the illustrated clip.

We used the transforEmotion R package to generate frame-level
classifier-output scores from the calibration videos
(\citeproc{ref-tomasevic2026TransforEmotion}{Tomašević et al., 2026}).
The scoring call used model key \texttt{oai-large}, which maps in the
local model registry to \texttt{openai/clip-vit-large-patch14},
corresponding to OpenAI CLIP ViT-Large/14. Each sampled video was
decoded into frames and scored against the prompt-defined
classifier-output channels labeled anger, disgust, fear, happiness,
neutral, sadness, surprise, contempt, anxiety, helplessness, and
disappointment. These 11 prompt channels follow the MAFW single-label
schema and are model outputs rather than frame-level human annotations.
Score vectors were closed to the simplex in the locked component order.
The primary calibration view retained complete rows; a sensitivity view
filled bounded internal gaps of at most two frames by interpolating the
whole ILR vector within a clip segment. These prompt-defined
classifier-output panels define empirical targets for the LOMM
observation layer through their temporal persistence, variability, and
co-fluctuation.

The selected frames from the same MAFW clip anchor the empirical example
(Panel A). Their classifier-output score profiles show that different
frames receive different values across channels labeled helplessness,
fear, disgust, sadness, anxiety, and disappointment (Panel B). The
frame-level trajectories show that the highest mean channels change in
bursts with irregular local movement (Panel C). The single-video DynEGA
network partitions the same clip's non-neutral classifier-output
trajectories (Panel D). This panel is an illustrative readout of
model-output channels in one clip; the simulation study later tests
recovery under a prespecified generating structure.

The empirical calibration sample was assembled at the clip level from a
label-diverse MAFW manifest, with MAFW category labels used for sample
diversity rather than validation targets. The primary complete-case view
contains 3,557 rows in 188 clip segments; usable clip lengths range from
16 to 50 frames, with a median of 35. The bounded-gap sensitivity view
interpolates whole ILR vectors across internal gaps of at most two
frames and contains 3,632 rows in 129 clip segments; usable clip lengths
range from 17 to 50 frames, with a median of 36.5. Both views retain 100
clips spanning the 11 MAFW single-label categories. The primary
complete-case view defines the adoption analysis, while the interpolated
view is a sensitivity analysis. The short clip lengths bound the
interpretation of autocorrelation and spectral summaries.

For the empirical realism check, empirical and simulated panels are
summarized at two levels. Observable-channel metrics are computed
directly from the classifier-output score series within each clip.
Recovered community-score summaries are computed after applying pooled
DynEGA to the score panel and extracting score summaries for the
recovered communities. This second readout is method-dependent
descriptive information, not independent evidence that the empirical
videos contain the same latent dimensions as the simulation. The
observable-channel readout evaluates channel-level amplitude and
movement; the recovered community-score readout evaluates the
lower-dimensional geometry induced by this DynEGA summarization step.

The primary realism targets summarize dispersion, local movement,
temporal structure, and effective multivariate dimensionality. Channel
IQR is the interquartile range of a score series, so higher values mean
wider score dispersion. Derivative IQR is the interquartile range of
first differences, so higher values mean larger frame-to-frame movement.
Lag-1 autocorrelation indexes local persistence. Run-length entropy is
the entropy of monotonic run lengths in the first-difference sign
series, so higher values indicate more varied directional regimes.
Spectral entropy is the normalized entropy of the fast-Fourier-transform
power distribution, so higher values indicate broader spectral
complexity. Effective rank is the entropy-based rank of the clip-level
correlation matrix, so higher values indicate more effective
multivariate dimensionality in the score panel.

For channel IQR, derivative IQR, lag-1 autocorrelation, run-length
entropy, and spectral entropy, the empirical and simulated distributions
contain one value for each clip-by-channel series or
clip-by-community-summary series. For effective rank, the distributions
contain one value for each clip panel. For each metric distribution, we
compare empirical and simulated values using a scaled median difference
and a scaled one-dimensional Wasserstein distance. Both discrepancies
are divided by the empirical interquartile range for that metric
distribution, with a standard-deviation fallback when the empirical
interquartile range is near zero. We describe the larger discrepancy as
small when it is at most 0.33 empirical interquartile ranges,
intermediate when it is above 0.33 and at most 1.00 interquartile range,
and large when it exceeds 1.00 interquartile range. The lower threshold
approximates one-third of the empirical interquartile range and
preserves the implemented value. These heuristic, scale-relative bands
make the magnitude easier to read; the continuous discrepancy and its
uncertainty remain the evidential quantities. The bands are neither
inferential cutoffs nor validation thresholds for the scoring model or
prompt labels.

The completed B/S/G realism ablation uses five clip-level folds assigned
deterministically within MAFW label and usable-length order. Each fold
holds out 20 clips. The contrast center, loading directions,
between-clip covariance, and oscillator candidate are estimated on the
remaining 80 clips and then held fixed. Candidate selection uses
observable probability and log-ratio discrepancies. The selected
calibration generates 100 evaluation panels for each held-out fold.
Clip-level metric contributions are retained for 2,000-resample
fold-stratified clip-cluster intervals; no held-out clip contributes to
calibration. The earlier revision-v2 calibration and recovered-community
summary remain a separate historical analysis.

\subsection{Scenario Selection}\label{scenario-selection}

Scenario selection links the empirical calibration targets to the
simulation design. We group manipulated conditions into three parameter
families (Panel D). The dynamics family varies the position feedback,
velocity feedback, and innovation variance parameters, \(\eta_f\),
\(\zeta_f\), and \(\sigma_{qf}\). The activity family varies whether one
latent expressive dimension dominates across the full sequence, whether
dominance switches after sampled dwell intervals, or whether all
dimensions remain close to low-expression background activity. In the
historical revision-v2 generator, the measurement family varies the
block loading matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_i\) and measurement-error
covariance matrix \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_i\) that generate the observed
classifier-output score panels.

The scenario set contains six activity-and-dynamics families. Four
families are generic score-panel regimes: low-expression single

dimensions times the number of indicators per dimension, \(p=mk\). The
matched three-method grid uses two- and three-dimensional generating
structures, and the broad DynEGA extension includes two-, three-, and
four-dimensional structures. The final crossed factors, replication
counts, and method-specific grid sizes are specified in the
dimensional-recovery simulation section below.

\subsection{Dimensional-Recovery Simulation
Study}\label{dimensional-recovery-simulation-study}

The simulation begins with latent trajectories and the prespecified
number of simulated expressive dimensions (Panel E). The LOMM
observation layer adds the declared clip baseline, applies clip scale to
the dynamic signal and residual, and returns either bounded compositions
or the identified contrast surface (Panel F). This produces a panel with
explicit profile and geometry metadata (Panel G). The final step
compares the estimated number of dimensions and item memberships with
the generating measurement structure (Panel H). The loading-aligned mode
is used when between-clip baselines must remain inside a known loading
space. The \texttt{none} mode with clip scale disabled is the exact
homogeneous profile nested within the public layer; revision-v2 remains
a separate historical generator.

The method comparison uses a common graph-partitioning target. Each
pipeline receives the same simulated score panel, estimates a structure
among observed score channels, converts that structure to communities,
and compares the resulting partition with the generating indicator
blocks. The target is recovery of LOMM-generated score blocks.
Classifier-label validity and method superiority for substantive emotion
inference are outside this test.

The matched three-pipeline design is the primary comparative analysis.
We compare Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis {[}DynEGA; H. Golino et
al. (\citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{2022}){]} with GraphicalVAR
(\citeproc{ref-epskampggm}{Epskamp et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-graphicalVAR2024}{Epskamp, 2024}) and GIMME
(\citeproc{ref-gates2012group}{Gates \& Molenaar, 2012};
\citeproc{ref-lane2019uncovering}{Lane et al., 2019}) because they
represent adjacent approaches to intensive longitudinal structure.
DynEGA is the focal dimensional-recovery method because it estimates
groups of variables whose changes are coordinated over time. In the
present simulation, DynEGA tests direct recovery of the generating score
blocks. GraphicalVAR is a dynamic-network comparator that separates
lagged temporal relations from contemporaneous partial associations, and
its fitted contemporaneous structure is partitioned after estimation.
GIMME is an indirect feasibility-stress comparator: it estimates
time-series paths for each clip treated as a separate time series, and
the resulting contemporaneous structures are aggregated and partitioned.
This use of GIMME treats clips as analytic series and should not be read
as participant-level idiographic affect modeling.

DynEGA combines time-delay embedding and derivative estimation from
dynamical systems with exploratory graph analysis for dimensionality
assessment (\citeproc{ref-glla2010}{Boker et al., 2010};
\citeproc{ref-golino2019investigating}{H. Golino et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-golino2017ega1}{H. F. Golino \& Epskamp, 2017}). We apply
DynEGA to the stacked video panel with video identity supplied as the
grouping variable. The embedding dimension is selected by minimizing the
Total Entropy Fit Index (TEFI) over candidate values
(\citeproc{ref-golino2019entropy}{H. Golino et al., 2021}), using
\(n_\mathrm{embed}\in\{5,10,15,\lfloor T/4\rfloor,\lfloor T/2\rfloor\}\)
where admissible. The LOMM generator retains the physical step
\(dt=0.4\) and uses it to calculate the observed horizon. The EGAnet
GLLA call records \texttt{delta\ =\ 1}, corresponding to one observation
interval. Expressing the derivatives per unit of physical time would
multiply every channel derivative by the common positive factor
\(1/dt\); this rescaling leaves the derivative correlation matrix and
its downstream EBICglasso network unchanged. The run artifacts record
physical \texttt{dt} and the DynEGA observation-unit \texttt{delta}
separately. We explicitly set \texttt{model\ =\ "glasso"} so DynEGA
estimates an EBICglasso regularized partial-correlation network from the
pooled derivative panel. That network is partitioned into communities
interpreted as recovered groups of score channels. The checked-in broad
grid uses Louvain community detection
(\citeproc{ref-blondel2008fast}{Blondel et al., 2008}) with the expand
rule for unidimensional solutions. The checked-in matched grid reports
Louvain, Leiden (\citeproc{ref-traag2019leiden}{Traag et al., 2019}),
and Walktrap (\citeproc{ref-pons2006walktrap}{Pons \& Latapy, 2005})
readouts.

GraphicalVAR estimates a graphical vector autoregressive model for
intensive longitudinal data (\citeproc{ref-epskampggm}{Epskamp et al.,
2018}; \citeproc{ref-graphicalVAR2024}{Epskamp, 2024}). In this
framework, the temporal network contains directed lag-1 regression
parameters, and the contemporaneous network contains partial
correlations among residuals at the same time point after lagged effects
have been taken into account. In multilevel applications, the model can
also distinguish within-person temporal and contemporaneous structure
from between-person associations among stationary means. We fit
GraphicalVAR to the stacked simulated score panel and use the estimated
contemporaneous partial-correlation matrix as the structural matrix for
dimensional partitioning, because LOMM recovery targets an undirected
grouping of score channels. The diagonal is ignored and the resulting
weighted matrix is treated as an undirected graph. Positive edge weights
define the graph, with absolute weights used only when no positive edges
are available. Louvain, Leiden, and Walktrap are applied to the same
fitted contemporaneous structure and are analyzed as the
community-detection factor.

GIMME is an automated time-series modeling approach built around unified
structural equation modeling and vector autoregressive representations
(\citeproc{ref-gates2012group}{Gates \& Molenaar, 2012};
\citeproc{ref-lane2019uncovering}{Lane et al., 2019}). The algorithm
searches for lagged and contemporaneous paths that improve fit across
the sample, retains paths that are common to many series, and then
allows subgroup-shared or series-specific paths. In this simulation,
each video clip is treated as the time series supplied to GIMME. We fit
the VAR specification to the video-level score panels, extract the
contemporaneous block from the clip-specific \(\boldsymbol{\Psi}\)
matrices, and average those blocks across clips to obtain a common
structural matrix for dimensional partitioning. The diagonal is ignored,
the matrix is symmetrized, and negative entries are set to zero. A
matrix with no remaining positive edge yields singleton communities
rather than an absolute-weight fallback. Louvain, Leiden, and Walktrap
are applied to the same fitted GIMME structure and are analyzed as the
community-detection factor.

The matched grid is the primary comparative design. All three fitted
structures are converted to dimensional partitions and evaluated against
the same prespecified generating structure. DynEGA is then evaluated on
the broader grid to assess scalability across the larger LOMM design
space. GraphicalVAR and GIMME are restricted to the matched grid because
complete factorial estimation is computationally unstable in this
setting. Community detection is a diagnostic factor in the matched
design; method and simulation design remain the primary organizing
variables. Appendix A derives the population structural signal supplied
by each pipeline and collects implementation differences in a common
perturbation framework.

The prospective revision specification locks the matched \(T=100\)
design to 21,600 generated panels: six scenario families, two generating
dimensionalities, two sample sizes, three measurement-error levels,
three loading levels, and 100 paired replications. Every method receives
a method-independent panel key, paired simulation seed, generator
specification hash, observed horizon, and stability classification.
DynEGA, GraphicalVAR, GIMME, and static EGA each supply one retained
structure per panel; Louvain at resolution 1.0 is the primary common
partition, and Leiden and Walktrap are paired sensitivity readouts of
that same structure. The true-block oracle passes the generating block
matrix through the identical partition helper. DynEGA additionally
retains its native expand readout and a correlation-Louvain readout at
resolution 0.95 as secondary paired analyses. GIMME's common structural
matrix is the positive part of the symmetrized mean contemporaneous
matrix,
\([(\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}^\top)/2]_+\).

The short-record study repeats the 21,600-panel design with \(T=25\),
\(dt=0.4\), and observed horizon \(H=9.6\), using DynEGA embedding
candidates \(\{5,6,10,12\}\) and seeds paired to the corresponding
\(T=100\) conditions. It is interpreted as a same-cadence,
shorter-horizon stress test. GIMME is designated as outside its
preferred design range in this condition, and its 900-second per-panel
budget is retained in the feasibility denominator. The separate
challenge suite crosses the six scenario families, two dimensionalities,
50 paired replications, and seven one-factor-at-a-time variants. It
fixes \(N=100\), \(T=100\), error standard deviation 0.25, loading 0.60,
and four indicators per factor; the variants address initialization,
smooth gates, cross-loadings, residual correlation, innovation
correlation, and bounded softmax scores represented both raw and by
centered log-ratios.

Performance is summarized using dimensional hit rate, assignment
accuracy for observed score channels, matched trajectory-recovery
correlation, matched trajectory-recovery normalized root mean squared
error (NRMSE), runtime, and failure or timeout accounting.
Method-readout rows are the denominator for dimensional hit rate and the
status counts in Table~\ref{tbl-method-overall}; they include one row
per method, generated panel, and community-detection readout. For
GraphicalVAR and GIMME, feasibility is also reported as unique
structure-fit status counts before the repeated Louvain, Leiden, and
Walktrap readouts. Dimensional hit rate is the proportion of
method-readout rows in which the method returns the correct number of
latent dimensions. Assignment accuracy is computed for readouts with
available partitions and asks whether observed score channels are
assigned to the correct generating blocks after optimal matching of
estimated communities to those blocks. For trajectory-recovery metrics,
observed variables are standardized within the full panel and recovered
community-score summaries are computed from the first principal
component of each estimated block, with row means used when a
principal-component score cannot be estimated. Matched
trajectory-recovery correlation and NRMSE compare these recovered score
summaries with the generating latent trajectories after sign-invariant
matching between estimated communities and generating dimensions. These
trajectory-recovery diagnostics are computed only when score summaries
are available; they are secondary diagnostics rather than
status-inclusive method-performance measures. Runtime is recorded for
each model fit.

The prospective result contract separates estimator feasibility from
recovery after a usable output. It reports \(P(\mathrm{usable})\),
\(P(\widehat m=m\mid\mathrm{usable})\), and
\(P(\mathrm{usable\ and\ correct})\); signed and absolute dimension
error; adjusted Rand index, variation of information, and assignment
accuracy conditional on a usable partition; marginal score correlation
and NRMSE; completion within 900 seconds; restricted compute time
\(\min(\mathrm{runtime},900)\); and the median and interquartile range
of runtime among successful fits. Method, horizon, oracle, and challenge
contrasts are panel-paired. Uncertainty uses 2,000 panel-cluster
resamples, accompanied by Monte Carlo standard errors. Error, timeout,
and partial rows remain in every status-inclusive denominator.

The final simulation uses a split design. The matched three-method grid
uses four indicators per latent dimension, two- and three-dimensional
generating structures, two sample sizes, 100 time points, three loading
strengths, three measurement-error standard deviations, and all six
scenario families. The reported final runs fix the innovation quality
scale at \(Q=0.75\), so the scenario base \(\sigma_q\) vectors listed in
the supplementary scenario-parameter tables are the final-run innovation
vectors. The latent systems were advanced with the exact linear
transition of Equation \ref{eq:lomm-latent} using step size \(dt=0.4\)
for 100-point series and \(dt=0.25\) for 300-point series, so
\(\sigma_{qf}\) is the per-step shock standard deviation at that
resolution. This matched grid contains 216 structural cells with 100
replications. Louvain, Leiden, and Walktrap are included as
community-detection levels, yielding 64,800 method-readout rows per
method in the matched three-method grid. The broad DynEGA extension
varies the number of videos, series length, latent dimensionality,
indicators per dimension, loading strength, and measurement-error
standard deviation across 1,296 structural cells with 400 replications,
yielding 518,400 DynEGA Louvain/expand method rows. Timeouts, failed

reporting denominator because they are part of method behavior in

\subsection{Computational Environment}\label{computational-environment}

Final run metadata records R 4.5.1 on Rocky Linux 8.8
(\texttt{x86\_64-redhat-linux-gnu}), 48 available logical cores,
OpenBLAS 0.3.15, and LAPACK 3.9.0. Local inspection of the execution
host for this final-results workspace reports dual Intel Xeon Silver
4310 CPUs, 48 logical threads, and 125 GiB RAM. The run artifacts store
the operating system, R session, and available-core count; they do not
store RAM or a separate hostname. Key package versions were EGAnet
2.3.0, graphicalVAR 0.3.4, gimme 10.0, igraph 2.2.1, qgraph 1.9.8, psych
2.5.6, lavaan 0.6-20, dplyr 1.1.4, and ggplot2 4.0.1. Parallel jobs used
one BLAS thread per worker. The broad DynEGA grid used 16 workers with
one internal DynEGA core per fit, the matched DynEGA community-detection
supplement used 32 workers, and the GraphicalVAR and GIMME matched-grid
runs each used 24 workers with a 900-second elapsed-time limit per
structure fit.

Runtime is elapsed wall-clock time, recorded with
\texttt{proc.time(){[}{[}"elapsed"{]}{]}} for successful fitted
structures and summarized in seconds. For GraphicalVAR and GIMME, the
recorded runtime includes the structure fit and post-fit
extraction/community-detection readout. The same fitted structure is
reused for Louvain, Leiden, and Walktrap, so feasibility accounting
separates unique structure-fit status from repeated method-readout rows.
Runtime summaries use successful fits. Timeout and error rows remain in
feasibility and recovery denominators. The runtime results should
therefore be read as within-machine feasibility estimates for this
simulation workflow rather than hardware-independent speed benchmarks.

not establish empirical validity for the historical linear recovery
panels or external validity for the current compositional default.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

LOMM gave a controlled score-generation problem for evaluating dynamic
dimensional recovery. The empirical-realism checks reproduced local
movement and directional-change texture, partially matched persistence,
spectral complexity, and effective multivariate dimensionality, and
retained a clear amplitude-dispersion mismatch. In the recovery grids,
DynEGA recovered the generating dimensionality and indicator blocks most
consistently in these LOMM simulation designs and completed the broad
focal-method grid. Recovery weakened when simulated measurement signal
was lower. GraphicalVAR was informative but condition-sensitive, and
GIMME was feasibility-limited in the matched design. The
trajectory-recovery diagnostics add a conditional result among
method-readout rows with recovered score summaries; they are not a
status-inclusive method ranking.

Appendix A frames recovery in the historical homogeneous revision-v2
linear benchmark panels as a method-specific graph perturbation problem.
Under that linear benchmark model, indicators assigned to the same
latent oscillator share a common change signal. Indicators assigned to
different oscillators are independent. The measurement signal within
each dimension is rank-one, and per-channel filtered population graphs
form disconnected weighted cliques under the symmetric block target.
DynEGA reads this structure through smoothed derivative estimates.
GraphicalVAR reaches the same block algebra through a multivariate VAR
innovation projection under the simplified population target. GIMME adds
an indirect graph-conversion and completion layer rather than a
closed-form population block edge. The population edge sets how much
common block signal reaches community detection, and recovery becomes
more difficult when the method-specific perturbation approaches the
weakest within-block edge.

DynEGA is closely aligned with this target in these LOMM simulation
designs. DynEGA groups variables whose estimated changes are coordinated
over time, so the common derivative signal within each LOMM block is the
relevant recovery signal. The high dimensional hit rate, high
block-assignment accuracy, and complete broad-grid coverage indicate
that this signal usually remained identifiable across the simulated
score-panel regimes. The reductions under weak loadings, high
measurement error, and low-expression generated regimes identify
conditions in which the observed channels no longer carry enough common
dynamic variation for reliable partitioning.

The generator separates three parts of the measurement problem. The
oscillator layer controls restoring forces, momentum, and stochastic
perturbation. The activity layer controls dominance and switching. The
observation layer controls loading geometry, measurement error,
between-clip baselines, clip scale, and the reported score surface. The
public default activates empirical B+S on the compositional surface;
loading-aligned and zero baselines remain explicit alternatives, and the
homogeneous model is nested at zero baseline and unit scale. This
separation lets method researchers test whether an analysis recovers
latent organization or responds mainly to features of the observation
system.

GraphicalVAR represents the generating measurement blocks less directly.
In this comparison, community detection is applied to the
contemporaneous partial-correlation matrix, which conditions each
pairwise association on the remaining channels. Appendix A shows that,
in the symmetric block model, the population VAR innovation graph
preserves block support but compresses the within-block edge toward a
ceiling set by block size. The VAR projection also removes the
predictable part of the common block signal before the contemporaneous
graph is read. The observed sensitivity of GraphicalVAR is consistent
with this mechanism, especially when loadings are weak or measurement
error is high. The simulation does not isolate this population
attenuation from lag estimation, regularization, finite-sample
variation, or timeout behavior. In the matched grid, GraphicalVAR
completed most structure fits and had 728 unique structure-fit timeouts,
so its main limitation in this comparison was condition sensitivity with
some timeout loss.

GIMME has broader native aims than dimensional partitioning. It searches
for group, subgroup, and individual lagged and contemporaneous paths. In
the present evaluation, video clips are analytic time series;
clip-specific contemporaneous structures are averaged and then
partitioned with a separate community-detection step. Its lower
status-inclusive hit rate therefore reflects both the indirect
GIMME-to-community graph and substantial feasibility costs, including
long successful-fit runtimes, timeouts, and post-estimation errors. The
result evaluates the implemented clip-level GIMME-to-community procedure
under this design. Person-level idiographic applications of GIMME
require separate evidence.

With a valid structural graph, trajectory-recovery diagnostics showed
the largest marginal differences by generated scenario family and
measurement-error standard deviation. Ranked-persistence and broad
switching-geometry panels produced the closest trajectory summaries.
Low-expression single-dominance panels were the most difficult. These
diagnostics evaluate recovery of simulated latent trajectories after a
usable structure has been estimated. They do not evaluate
classifier-output validity, emotion-label validity, or status-inclusive
method performance. Full marginal summaries are reported in Appendix C.

The empirical realism evidence limits the interpretation to selected
statistical resemblance between simulated panels and classifier-output
score panels from the retained MAFW calibration sample. It cannot
validate the scoring model, the prompt labels, or subjective emotional
states in the videos. The calibration used one dataset label schema and
one scoring implementation, and the empirical clips were short relative
to some simulation regimes. The short empirical clips support
calibration of selected local score-series properties. The longer
simulation conditions test method behavior under controlled series
lengths. B was the clearest descriptive improvement over the homogeneous
observation profile in the paired ablation; S produced a smaller and
less certain marginal improvement, and B+S was retained as the practical
calibrated default. The fold-stratified clip-cluster percentile
intervals are descriptive, including the bootstrap-bias boundaries for
the nonlinear B and S estimands. This default remains configurable and
requires evaluation on other scoring systems and datasets.

The comparison is narrow by design. The broad method-comparison results
apply to the historical homogeneous linear block-measurement panels. The
paired bridge screens loading-aligned B+S on pre-link linear panels in
selected stable \(T=100\) conditions; it does not evaluate recovery from
bounded probabilities or establish equivalence across the full benchmark
grid. For the primary Louvain status-inclusive usable-and-correct
outcome, DynEGA was 0.750 under loading-aligned B+S and 0.775 under
homogeneous, a paired effect of \(-0.025\) with a 95\% percentile
interval of \([-0.100,0.050]\). GIMME was 0.400 under both profiles,
GraphicalVAR was 0.0375 under both, and the oracle was 1.000 under both;
static EGA increased from 0.275 to 1.000. This was a compatibility
screen rather than an equivalence test: the DynEGA interval remains
compatible with a 0.10 loss, every three-dimensional GIMME fit timed out
under both profiles, and the conditional GIMME partition metrics contain
40 paired usable two-dimensional fits. GraphicalVAR's zero primary
dimensional contrast co-occurred with declines in Louvain adjusted Rand
index (\(-0.0226\), 95\% interval \([-0.0484,-0.0013]\)) and assignment
accuracy (\(-0.0198\), 95\% interval \([-0.0391,-0.0026]\)). These
contrasts do not establish adequate absolute recovery. The present LOMM
implementation uses independent oscillator dimensions, activity-gated
latent trajectories, and configurable observation geometry. These
choices keep the recovery target interpretable while leaving richer
dependence structures for separate tests. The method comparison covers
network-based or network-convertible recovery pipelines. Other dynamic
latent-variable approaches remain outside the present evaluation. Future
work should test richer oscillator coupling, cross-loadings, correlated
measurement error, additional compositional scoring systems, and
empirical applications where simulation results guide method validation
before substantive interpretation of classifier-output score dynamics.
The present results support LOMM as a controlled validation environment
for selected temporal and dimensional properties. Full generative
theories of affect and validation of scoring-model emotion labels
require separate evidence.

\section{Appendix A: Perturbation Targets for Historical Revision-v2
Linear
Recovery}\label{appendix-a-perturbation-targets-for-historical-revision-v2-linear-recovery}

Appendix A explains why the historical homogeneous revision-v2
block-measured linear panel gives different recovery margins for DynEGA,
GraphicalVAR, and GIMME. This linear panel can be written as a graph
perturbation problem: each method supplies a population structural
signal for the same score panel, and implementation adds finite-sample,
estimation, convergence, extraction, and community-detection
perturbations.

The argument has three claims. First, idealized DynEGA and GraphicalVAR
population graphs preserve the generating block support exactly under
the symmetric linear block target. Second, their within-block
partial-correlation edges share one ceiling; their difference comes from
the signal-to-noise ratio delivered by derivative or VAR innovation
filtering. Third, GIMME has a different mathematical status because
recovery depends on path search, convergence, extraction, averaging,
graph conversion, and completion. The sections below define the target,
derive the two population graph maps, state the perturbation margin, and
summarize the method comparison. This appendix does not derive recovery
for empirical-baseline compositions or CLR panels. The loading-aligned
bridge adds between-clip mean structure outside the zero-mean population
target derived here.

\subsection{A.1 Linear Block Target}\label{a.1-linear-block-target}

Consider the population target for the historical homogeneous
revision-v2 linear benchmark, with \(m\) latent dimensions, \(k\)
observed indicators per dimension, and \(p=mk\) observed score channels.
Let \(B_1,\ldots,B_m\) denote the true indicator blocks. For indicator
\(j \in B_f\), use the equal-loading, equal-error measurement model

\[
Y_j(t)=\lambda Z_f(t)+\varepsilon_j(t),
\qquad
Z_f(t)=a_f(t)X_f(t),
\]

where \(Z_f(t)\) is the activity-scaled latent oscillator for dimension
\(f\), \(\lambda>0\), and \(\varepsilon_j(t)\) is measurement error with
variance \(\psi=s_\varepsilon^2\). Measurement errors are independent
across indicators and time in this target, so
\(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_j(t),\varepsilon_\ell(s))=0\) whenever
\(j\neq \ell\) or \(t\neq s\). The latent processes \(Z_f\) are
zero-mean and mutually independent in this simplified population object.
Activity gating makes \(Z_f\) nonstationary by design, so the variances
used in this appendix are time-averaged second moments over the analyzed
window. Loading jitter, item-level variance jitter, switching schedules,
initialization transients, finite-sample activity imbalance, and
estimator behavior are treated as perturbations around this symmetric
target.

For a method readout \(r\), write the fitted graph as

\[
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^{(r)}
=
\mathbf{W}_*^{(r)}+\mathbf{E}^{(r)}.
\]

Here \(\mathbf{W}_*^{(r)}\) is the population structural matrix induced
by the method readout, and \(\mathbf{E}^{(r)}\) collects finite-sample
variation, derivative estimation, model estimation, regularization,
convergence behavior, extraction errors, and community-detection
effects. This notation keeps the comparison focused on two quantities:
the population signal available to the method and the perturbation added
by the implemented pipeline.

\subsection{A.2 Filtered Panels and Two Graph
Readouts}\label{a.2-filtered-panels-and-two-graph-readouts}

The derivation uses two population graph maps for a per-channel filtered
block panel. DynEGA estimates local derivatives of every channel, and a
lag difference is a simple analytic example of the same channelwise
operation. GraphicalVAR is handled separately in A.3 because its
contemporaneous innovations come from a multivariate VAR projection
rather than from an identical filter applied to each channel.

Let \(L\) denote a linear filter applied to each channel separately.
Applying \(L\) to the measurement model for \(j \in B_f\) gives

\[
(LY_j)(t)=\lambda\,(LZ_f)(t)+(L\varepsilon_j)(t),
\]

so the filtered panel keeps the one-factor block structure. Write
\(V_{L,f}=\operatorname{Var}(LZ_f)\) for the filtered common-signal
variance and \(\psi_L=\operatorname{Var}(L\varepsilon_j)\) for the
filtered noise variance. Because \(L\) acts on each channel separately,
filtered errors stay independent across channels, and the within-block
covariance matrix of the filtered channels is

\[
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{L,f}
=
\lambda^{2}V_{L,f}\,\mathbf{1}_k\mathbf{1}_k^\top+\psi_L I_k,
\qquad
\theta_{L,f}=\frac{\lambda^{2}V_{L,f}}{\psi_L},
\]

with zero cross-block covariance. The filtered signal-to-noise ratio
\(\theta_{L,f}\) is the only quantity through which the filter enters
the population graph. Filtered errors can be serially correlated; the
population readouts below use only the contemporaneous covariance across
channels, and serial dependence is absorbed into the estimation
perturbation in A.4.

The filtered panel admits two graph readouts. The marginal readout uses
correlations directly. For two distinct indicators \(j,\ell \in B_f\),

\[
w^{M}_f(L)
=
\left|\operatorname{Cor}(LY_j,LY_\ell)\right|
=
\frac{\theta_{L,f}}{1+\theta_{L,f}}.
\]

The partial readout conditions each pair on the remaining channels.
Using the Sherman-Morrison identity,

\[
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{L,f}^{-1}
=
\psi_L^{-1}
\left[
I_k-
\frac{\theta_{L,f}}{1+k\theta_{L,f}}
\mathbf{1}_k\mathbf{1}_k^\top
\right],
\]

and the absolute partial correlation between two indicators in the same
block is

\[
w^{P}_f(L)
=
\frac{\theta_{L,f}}{1+(k-1)\theta_{L,f}}.
\]

Because the filtered precision matrix is block-diagonal, cross-block
partial correlations are exactly zero. These marginal and partial
readouts therefore preserve the generating block support without error
for the per-channel filtered population object, and the population graph
has block-clique form for any per-channel filter,

\[
\mathbf{W}_*
=
\operatorname{blockdiag}
\left(
w_1(J_k-I_k),\ldots,w_m(J_k-I_k)
\right),
\qquad
w_f\in\{w^{M}_f(L),\,w^{P}_f(L)\}.
\]

The two readouts differ in how much within-block signal they keep. For
the same signal-to-noise ratio \(\theta\),

\[
\frac{w^P(\theta)}{w^M(\theta)}
=
\frac{1+\theta}{1+(k-1)\theta},
\]

so \(w^P(\theta)<w^M(\theta)\) whenever \(k>2\) and \(\theta>0\). As
\(\theta \to \infty\),

\[
w^P(\theta)\to \frac{1}{k-1},
\qquad
w^M(\theta)\to 1.
\]

The partial readout compresses an arbitrarily strong common-source
signal toward a ceiling set by block size alone: with four indicators
per dimension, no within-block partial-correlation edge can exceed
\(1/3\). Both readout maps are increasing in \(\theta\), so filter and
readout separate cleanly. The filter sets \(\theta_{L,f}\), and the
readout maps \(\theta_{L,f}\) to the edge weight supplied to community
detection.

Figure~\ref{fig-readout-curves} compares the marginal and partial maps
for the matched grid's four indicators per dimension. The marginal map
is a pre-conditioning benchmark; neither implemented pipeline uses it.
The vertical gap between the curves shows how much compression the
partial map applies to the common-source signal. The shaded band marks
the \(\lambda^2/s_\varepsilon^2\) span of the matched measurement grid,
so the simulated loading and error conditions place \(\theta_{L,f}\)
inside the band up to the filtered common-signal variance \(V_{L,f}\).
Across that span, the marginal curve rises from mid-range values toward
1. The partial curve is already near its \(1/3\) ceiling: strong
measurement conditions widen the common-source signal margin but cannot
widen the partial-correlation edge beyond the ceiling.

\subsection{A.3 Method-Specific Filters and Signal-to-Noise
Ratios}\label{a.3-method-specific-filters-and-signal-to-noise-ratios}

The implemented pipelines are specific readout combinations. DynEGA
estimates generalized local linear approximation (GLLA) derivatives of
each score channel over a time-delay embedding window and then estimates
a regularized partial-correlation network (EBICglasso) on the derivative
panel. The implementation uses one observation interval as the GLLA
\texttt{delta} and retains the generator's physical \(dt=0.4\) in
separate horizon metadata. Conversion to physical-time derivatives
applies the same positive scale factor to every channel and therefore
leaves the derivative correlation readout unchanged. DynEGA's population
object is the partial readout of a per-channel derivative filter,
\(w^{P}_f(L_{\mathrm{GLLA}})\). A lag-\(h\) difference,
\(\Delta_hY_j(t)=Y_j(t+h)-Y_j(t)\), gives a transparent analytic
stand-in for that filter, with

\[
\theta^{\Delta}_f
=
\frac{\lambda^2\operatorname{Var}(\Delta_hZ_f)}{2\psi},
\]

because independent measurement errors contribute \(2\psi\) to the
variance of every differenced channel. Both the lag difference and GLLA
derivative estimation operate channel by channel. For the smooth
oscillator trajectories generated here, the GLLA filter improves on this
stand-in: it averages frame-level measurement noise over the embedding
window while tracking the common latent change signal, which supports a
high filtered signal-to-noise ratio.

GraphicalVAR reads partial correlations from the contemporaneous
innovations of a lag-1 vector autoregression, so its population object
is the partial readout of a multivariate VAR innovation projection,
\(w^{P}_f(L_{\mathrm{VAR}})\). For the population lag-1 fit of the
symmetric block model, the implied coefficient matrix is proportional to
\(\mathbf{1}_k\mathbf{1}_k^\top\) within each block, and the innovation
covariance retains the one-factor-plus-diagonal form. The compression
map of A.2 therefore applies at the innovation scale under this
symmetric target. The VAR projection also removes the predictable part
of the common signal by construction: the simulated oscillator
trajectories are strongly autocorrelated, so the lagged panel explains
much of the common variance, while the serially independent measurement
error passes into the innovations. The innovation readout therefore
tends to enter the shared compression map with a smaller signal-to-noise
ratio than a derivative filter applied to the same panel.

The population comparison between DynEGA and GraphicalVAR then has a
precise form. Both population readouts supply partial-correlation edges
to community detection, so both within-block edges are capped at
\(1/(k-1)\) and the generating block support is preserved in both
population objects under the symmetric target. The population difference
is where the derivative and innovation signal-to-noise ratios
\(\theta_{\mathrm{GLLA}}\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm{VAR}}\) place each edge
below that shared ceiling. The remaining differences are pipeline
perturbations, which A.4 collects in a single margin.

\subsection{A.4 Shared Perturbation
Margin}\label{a.4-shared-perturbation-margin}

Let a method's population graph have block-clique form

\[
\mathbf{W}_*^{(r)}
=
\operatorname{blockdiag}
\left(
w_1^{(r)}(J_k-I_k),\ldots,w_m^{(r)}(J_k-I_k)
\right),
\]

where edge weights are on the nonnegative scale supplied to community
detection and all \(m\) blocks share the same size \(k\). For DynEGA and
GraphicalVAR, \(w_f^{(r)}=w^{P}_f(L_r)\) with the method-specific filter
from A.3. Suppose every off-diagonal edge is estimated within
\(\delta_r\) of its readout-specific population value:

\[
\max_{j\neq \ell}
\left|
\widehat{W}^{(r)}_{j\ell}-W^{(r)}_{*,j\ell}
\right|
\leq \delta_r.
\]

For any node \(j \in B_f\), the estimated total connection to its true
block is at least

\[
(k-1)(w_f^{(r)}-\delta_r),
\]

whereas its estimated total connection to any wrong block is at most

\[
k\delta_r.
\]

Thus each node has stronger total connection to its true block than to
any wrong block whenever

\[
(k-1)(w_{\min}^{(r)}-\delta_r)>k\delta_r,
\]

or equivalently

\[
\delta_r
<
\frac{k-1}{2k-1}w_{\min}^{(r)}.
\]

For the matched grid with \(k=4\), the sufficient node-level
block-preference and threshold-separation condition is

\[
\delta_r < \frac{3}{7}w_{\min}^{(r)}.
\]

This margin is not a guarantee that Louvain, Leiden, or Walktrap recover
the blocks, because exact recovery depends on the implemented
community-detection algorithm. The bound has a narrower consequence.
Because \(\tfrac{k-1}{2k-1}<\tfrac{1}{2}\), the condition forces every
estimated cross-block edge below every estimated within-block edge, so
any threshold \(\tau\in(\delta_r,\,w_{\min}^{(r)}-\delta_r)\) deletes
all cross-block edges, keeps each block a connected clique, and
reproduces the generating blocks exactly as the connected components of
the thresholded graph.

The margin treats perturbation as symmetric noise on every edge, which
makes \(\delta_r\) a worst-case envelope. Regularized estimation acts
asymmetrically: glasso shrinkage lowers within-block edges, which
reduces \(w_{\min}^{(r)}\), and sets weak cross-block edges to zero,
which supports block separation.

The two population partial-readout pipelines share the same ceiling.
DynEGA and GraphicalVAR supply partial-correlation edges to community
detection, so \(w_{\min}^{(r)}\leq 1/(k-1)\) for both pipelines, and
with \(k=4\) the sufficient perturbation margin approaches at most

\[
\delta_r
<
\frac{3}{7}\cdot\frac{1}{3}
=
\frac{1}{7}
\]

in the high-signal limit. The population comparison therefore reduces to
the two quantities identified in A.3: how close each filtered
signal-to-noise ratio keeps \(w_{\min}^{(r)}\) to the shared ceiling,
and how large each pipeline's realized perturbation \(\delta_r\) is. The
observed recovery ordering is consistent with the derivative filter
preserving more within-block signal than the innovation filter. The full
graphical vector-autoregression fit also adds larger estimation and
timeout perturbations.

The margin also identifies which blocks are fragile in each scenario
family. Activity gating enters the filtered signal directly: \(V_{L,f}\)
is the time-averaged variance of the filtered activity-scaled process,
so a dimension held at background gain contributes a small
\(\theta_{L,f}\) and a weak within-block edge. In the single-dominance
families, background dimensions run at gains of 0.05--0.08 against
active gains of 0.35--1.25 (Appendix E), so \(w_{\min}^{(r)}\) is set by
the background blocks, and in the low-expression family every dimension
sits near background activity. The margin therefore predicts the
scenario-family ordering observed in Figure~\ref{fig-dimension-recovery}
for DynEGA and GraphicalVAR: recovery is hardest where the activity
schedule holds the weakest block's signal lowest. For GIMME, the same
activity envelope is an expected stressor, but its effect is mediated by
path search, convergence, extraction, averaging, graph conversion, and
completion.

\subsection{A.5 GIMME as an Indirect Graph-Conversion
Readout}\label{a.5-gimme-as-an-indirect-graph-conversion-readout}

The implemented GIMME comparator has a different mathematical status.
Its readout can be summarized as

\[
\widehat{\mathbf{W}}^G
=
\Pi
\left(
\frac{1}{V}\sum_{i=1}^{V}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_i
\right),
\]

where \(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_i\) is the clip-specific
contemporaneous structure extracted from GIMME for clip \(i\), \(V\) is
the number of clips, and \(\Pi(\cdot)\) denotes conversion to the
undirected graph supplied to community detection. This readout depends
on path search, clip-level model selection, convergence, extraction,
averaging, and graph conversion. We therefore summarize GIMME as an
indirect graph-conversion pipeline without a closed-form block edge
\(w_f^G\).

Status-inclusive recovery also includes completion. For any pipeline,

\[
\Pr(\mathrm{hit})
=
\Pr(\mathrm{usable\ graph})
\Pr(\mathrm{hit}\mid \mathrm{usable\ graph}).
\]

The reported feasibility tables make this decomposition concrete. DynEGA
returned usable graphs for every broad-grid and matched-grid row, so its
status-inclusive and conditional hit rates coincide at 95.3\%.
GraphicalVAR completed 20,872 of 21,600 matched-grid structure fits
(62,616 of 64,800 readout rows), so its 80.3\% status-inclusive hit rate
corresponds to a conditional hit rate of about 83\% among usable
readouts. GIMME completed 15,294 of 21,600 structure fits (45,882 of
64,800 readout rows), so its 25.9\% status-inclusive hit rate
corresponds to a conditional hit rate of about 37\%. The decomposition
separates GIMME's two losses: many fits return no usable graph, and
partition quality after a usable graph remains well below the other
pipelines.
