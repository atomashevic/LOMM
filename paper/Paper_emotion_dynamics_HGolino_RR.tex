\begin{abstract}
Facial-expression classifiers convert video of a person into
multivariate time series of classifier scores. These scores contain
measurement error from the classifier, the video, and the recording
conditions, and the latent structure that generated them is unknown.
Empirical score series alone therefore cannot show whether the score
channels are indicators of a smaller number of latent expressive
processes, or whether an analysis method would recover those processes
if they existed. We introduce the Latent Oscillator Measurement Model
(LOMM), a data-generating model that specifies three components
separately: latent dynamics as damped, undamped, or amplifying linear
oscillators; a time-varying activity level that determines how strongly
each latent dimension is expressed; and a factor-analytic observation
model with fallible indicators. Study 1 compared simulated score series
with classifier scores from 100 facial-expression video clips using
five-fold cross-fitting, in which parameters were estimated on training
clips and discrepancies were evaluated on held-out clips. Allowing clips
to differ in baseline and scale reduced the composite discrepancy by
21.0\%, from 0.3250 to 0.2567. Study 2 generated continuous indicators
with a known number of dimensions and known indicator assignments and
asked whether four methods, Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis (DynEGA),
static EGA, GraphicalVAR, and GIMME, recovered that structure through a
common community-detection step. At 100 observations per clip, with
failed or timed-out fits counted as incorrect, overall correct-dimension
recovery was 0.939 for DynEGA, 0.884 for static EGA, 0.777 for
GraphicalVAR, and 0.176 for GIMME. Replacing a shared initial state with
independent initial states reduced recovery for DynEGA, static EGA, and
GraphicalVAR, so a shared initial state overstates recovery. LOMM lets
researchers test whether a method recovers a known structure before
empirical score patterns are interpreted psychologically.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Facial-expression classifiers convert video of a person into
multivariate time series. Each frame yields a vector of scores, one for
each label in a chosen label set, at a temporal resolution that manual
coding cannot match.

Earlier classifiers returned scores only for the categories in their
training data. Vision-language models such as CLIP can score any label
supplied as text (\citeproc{ref-foteinopoulou2023EmoCLIP}{Foteinopoulou
\& Patras, 2023}; \citeproc{ref-li2023CLIPER}{Li et al., 2023};
\citeproc{ref-radford2021LearningTransferable}{Radford et al., 2021}).
The scores are nonetheless measurements: they depend on the model, the
label set, the video-processing pipeline, image quality, and recording
conditions, and their accuracy varies with model adaptation, label
choice, and evaluation data
(\citeproc{ref-foteinopoulou2023EmoCLIP}{Foteinopoulou \& Patras, 2023};
\citeproc{ref-li2023CLIPER}{Li et al., 2023}). A pattern among score
channels may therefore reflect expressive behavior, the measurement
process, or both. In this paper, classifier scores are treated as
fallible measurements of visible expression, not as measures of
subjective emotional experience.

This raises a psychometric question. Score channels can be analyzed as
separate variables, or they can be treated as fallible indicators of a
smaller number of latent expressive processes
(\citeproc{ref-mehu2015Emotion}{Mehu \& Scherer, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2002}{Nesselroade et al., 2002};
\citeproc{ref-russellCoreAffect2003}{Russell, 2003}). Measurement error
changes the structure that dynamic methods recover
(\citeproc{ref-kankaanpaa2026multipleindicator}{Kankaanpää et al.,
2026}). Empirical data cannot settle the question, because the true
number of latent dimensions and the true assignment of indicators to
dimensions are unknown, so there is no way to check whether a method
recovered them.

We introduce the Latent Oscillator Measurement Model (LOMM) to make that
check possible. LOMM is a data-generating model for multivariate
intensive longitudinal data with an explicit measurement model. It
simulates latent processes that change over time, specifies how strongly
each process is expressed at each observation, and maps the processes
onto fallible observed indicators. The number of dimensions, the
indicator assignments, the dynamics, the activity pattern, the loadings,
and the measurement error are all fixed before a recovery method is
applied. LOMM combines the damped linear oscillator model of
intraindividual dynamics (\citeproc{ref-boker2015Adaptive}{Boker, 2015};
\citeproc{ref-boker2002Method}{Boker \& Nesselroade, 2002}) with a
factor-analytic observation model
(\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2002}{Nesselroade et al., 2002};
\citeproc{ref-nesselroade2008dfm}{Zhang et al., 2008}).

We use LOMM in two studies (Figure~\ref{fig-pipeline}). Study 1 compares
simulated score series with classifier scores from 100 clips in MAFW, a
facial-expression video database (\citeproc{ref-liu_mafw_2022}{Liu et
al., 2022}), and asks whether eight descriptive features of the
simulated and real series are similar. Study 2 uses LOMM to generate
continuous indicators with a known factor structure and asks whether
four methods recover the number of dimensions and the indicator
assignments: Dynamic Exploratory Graph Analysis (DynEGA), static EGA,
GraphicalVAR, and GIMME. Static EGA ignores temporal order and is
computationally inexpensive; it shows how much structure is recoverable
from contemporaneous indicator levels alone. Study 2 also examines two
conditions that limit the main result: whether a shared initial state
across dimensions inflates recovery, and whether the indicator grouping
is preserved after the indicators are transformed to bounded scores that
sum to one, as classifier scores do. Because the empirical clips are
short (median 35 observations), Study 2 also reports a secondary
analysis with 25 observations per clip.

\section{The Latent Oscillator Measurement
Model}\label{the-latent-oscillator-measurement-model}

LOMM represents a standard psychometric situation: several observed
variables are fallible indicators of a smaller number of latent
processes, and those processes change over time. As an illustration,
suppose a simulation has three latent dimensions labeled
approach-affiliation, threat-defense, and orienting-novelty, and the
anger, fear, disgust, and anxiety channels are assigned as indicators of
threat-defense. Those four channels would covary over time because they
load on the same latent dimension, and each would also carry
channel-specific measurement error. A recovery method should group the
four channels together and should find three dimensions. These labels
only illustrate a possible simulation design; whether MAFW scores have
this structure is not tested here.

LOMM generates data in three layers. The latent layer describes how each
latent dimension changes over time. The activity layer determines how
strongly that dimension is expressed at each observation. The
observation layer creates multiple indicators with prespecified loadings
and measurement error. Keeping these layers separate allows a researcher
to change the dynamics, the expression pattern, or the measurement
quality while holding the other components fixed.

\emph{Notation.} Clips are indexed by \(i=1,\ldots,N\) and observations
within a clip by \(n=1,\ldots,T\), at times \(t_n=(n-1)\Delta\), where
\(\Delta\) is the observation interval. Latent dimensions are indexed by
\(f=1,\ldots,m\). Continuous indicators are indexed by \(j=1,\ldots,p\);
in Study 2, \(p=mk\) with \(k\) indicators per dimension. Bounded scores
have \(J\) channels. Bold symbols are vectors or matrices.

For clip \(i\) and latent dimension \(f\), let \(x_{if}(t)\) denote the
latent level and \(\dot{x}_{if}(t)\) its rate of change. LOMM treats
each dimension as a linear second-order oscillator:

\begin{equation}
\ddot{x}_{if}(t)
=
\eta_f x_{if}(t)
+
\zeta_f\dot{x}_{if}(t)
+
q_{if,n},
\qquad t\in[t_n,t_{n+1}).
\label{eq:lomm-latent}
\end{equation}

Equation 1 is the damped linear oscillator used in research on
intraindividual dynamics (\citeproc{ref-boker2002Method}{Boker \&
Nesselroade, 2002}). The position-feedback parameter \(\eta_f\)
determines the strength of the restoring force toward equilibrium; more
negative values produce faster oscillation. The velocity-feedback
parameter \(\zeta_f\) determines damping: negative values damp the
motion, zero leaves it undamped, and positive values amplify it. The
term \(q_{if,n}\) is a random acceleration input, drawn independently
for each interval between observations \(n\) and \(n+1\) and held
constant within that interval; it produces irregular peaks and reversals
in the trajectory. When \(\eta_f\) and \(\zeta_f\) are both negative,
the trajectory returns toward equilibrium, with oscillation when the
characteristic roots are complex. In the conditions tested here,
positive \(\zeta_f\) produces amplitudes that grow over the observed
record; we call this finite-horizon amplification because the record
ends before the growth becomes extreme. Appendix A gives the exact
discrete-time transition used to advance the system between
observations, the stability conditions, and the initialization rules.

The activity layer multiplies the latent level at each observation by a
nonnegative activity value, \(\tilde{x}_{if,n}=a_{if,n}x_{if,n}\). A
latent dimension can therefore remain strongly expressed throughout a
clip, remain near a low background level, or alternate with other
dimensions. This layer matters for recovery because an abrupt change in
activity produces a large change in the observed indicators even when
the oscillator itself has not accelerated.

The observation layer is a standard factor model:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf z_{in}
=
\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{in}
+
\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in},
\qquad
\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma_i).
\label{eq:lomm-measurement}
\end{equation}

Row \(j\) of \(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\) contains the loadings of indicator
\(j\) on the \(m\) latent dimensions, and
\(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}\) is measurement error with covariance
\(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\). Appendix A gives the complete observation
equation, including optional clip-specific baseline and scale terms.

LOMM can output two types of observed variables. The first is the vector
of continuous indicators \(\mathbf z_{in}\), used in the primary Study 2
designs; there, the factor structure that generated the data is defined
directly on the analyzed variables. The second is a vector of \(J\)
positive scores that sum to one, which matches the form of classifier
output and is used in Study 1. To produce bounded scores, LOMM maps the
latent dimensions to a \((J-1)\)-dimensional vector of log-ratio
coordinates and applies the softmax function. Because scores that sum to
one are compositional data, they are analyzed both as scores and in
log-ratio coordinates, which remove the sum constraint. A separate Study
2 condition applies the softmax directly to the \(p\) continuous
indicators and tests whether their original grouping is preserved.
Appendix A gives the softmax, log-ratio, and identification details.

In this paper, LOMM is used only to generate data with known
dimensionality and indicator assignments; it is not fitted to the MAFW
videos. Study 1 estimates the distribution of clip baselines and scales
from training clips. The primary Study 2 design sets clip baselines to
zero and scales to one but lets loadings and residual variances vary
slightly across clips. Estimating oscillator, activity, and measurement
parameters from empirical data would require the sign, scale,
temperature, and frequency constraints stated in Appendix A.

\section{Study 1: Comparing Simulated and Real Score
Series}\label{study-1-comparing-simulated-and-real-score-series}

Study 1 asks whether LOMM can reproduce selected statistical features of
real classifier-score series. The real series come from 100 clips in
MAFW, a large multimodal database of facial-expression video recorded in
naturalistic settings (\citeproc{ref-liu_mafw_2022}{Liu et al., 2022}).
Frames were scored with the transforEmotion R package
(\citeproc{ref-tomasevic2026TransforEmotion}{Tomašević et al., 2026})
using OpenAI's CLIP ViT-Large/14 model (setting \texttt{oai-large}),
with up to 50 frames sampled per clip. Eleven labels from the MAFW label
set served as the text prompts: anger, disgust, fear, happiness,
neutral, sadness, surprise, contempt, anxiety, helplessness, and
disappointment. For each frame, the classifier returns one score per
label, normalized to sum to one. These eleven scores are the channels
analyzed here. They are classifier outputs; nothing in the dataset or
the scoring procedure validates them as measures of subjective emotion.

Figure~\ref{fig-mafwvideo} shows the score series for one clip. Frames
without a detected face have missing scores, so each clip was divided
into segments of consecutive scored frames. The main analysis contains
3,557 observations in 188 segments from the 100 clips; segment lengths
range from 16 to 50 observations, with a median of 35. Because segments
are short, autocorrelation and spectral statistics are estimated
imprecisely. A sensitivity analysis interpolated internal gaps of at
most two frames, producing longer segments. Appendix B gives the
face-detection, score-validity, missing-data, and sampling details.

Study 1 compares eight LOMM configurations, formed by including or
excluding each of three features: clip-specific baseline, clip-specific
scale, and a brief transition in activity. Each configuration is
compared with the real data on eight summary statistics. Six are
computed for each score channel within each clip: median level;
interquartile range of levels; interquartile range of
observation-to-observation changes; lag-1 autocorrelation; entropy of
the distribution of monotonic run lengths, which summarizes how often
the series changes direction; and normalized spectral entropy, which is
low when variance is concentrated at a few frequencies and high when it
is spread across many. Two are computed across channels within a clip:
effective rank, which summarizes how many independent components the
channels contain, and total variance. All eight statistics are computed
on the scores and on their log-ratio coordinates, giving 16 comparisons.

For each statistic and each representation, discrepancy is computed from
the distributions of the statistic across clips in the simulated and
real data, in three steps. First, two distances are computed: the
absolute difference between the simulated and real medians, and the
Wasserstein distance between the two distributions, which measures how
far one distribution must be shifted to match the other. Second, both
distances are standardized by the spread of the real values, and the
larger is retained. Third, the eight standardized distances are averaged
within each representation, and the score and log-ratio averages are
weighted equally in the composite. Smaller values indicate closer
agreement.

The 100 clips were divided into five folds of 20 clips. Within each
fold, the measurement parameters (coordinate center, loading directions
and scales, and the covariance of clip baselines) were estimated from
the 80 training clips, and the oscillator period, damping ratio, and
observation scale were selected from a small grid using training clips
only. Each selected configuration then generated 100 simulated datasets
matching the held-out clips' observation indices and segment structure,
and discrepancy was computed on the held-out clips. Because the same
held-out folds were used both to compare the eight configurations and to
select the best one, the reported discrepancy of the selected
configuration is optimistically biased. The analysis is therefore an
exploratory comparison, not an external validation. Appendix B gives the
complete discrepancy, calibration, search, and bootstrap procedures.

\section{Study 2: Recovery of Dimensionality and Indicator
Assignments}\label{study-2-recovery-of-dimensionality-and-indicator-assignments}

\emph{Data-generating conditions.} Study 2 asks whether four
network-based methods recover a prespecified number of dimensions and a
prespecified assignment of indicators to dimensions. The primary
analyses use continuous indicators generated by LOMM. Each dataset
contains \(N\) clips with \(T\) observations each and \(p=mk\)
indicators. All clips in a dataset share the same indicator assignments
and the same nominal loadings and residual variances; each clip's
loadings and residual variances are perturbed by small random amounts.
Datasets are generated under six scenario families
(Table~\ref{tbl-scenario-words}), which differ in how strongly and how
persistently each latent dimension is expressed and in whether the
oscillator dynamics are stable or amplifying. Appendix B gives the exact
activity schedules and parameters.

Every method receives the same simulated dataset. Each method estimates
its own structure, that structure is converted to an undirected network
by a prespecified rule, and the same community-detection procedure
identifies groups of indicators. The estimated grouping is then compared
with the assignment used to generate the data. This creates a common
recovery target while preserving each method's own statistical model and
data handling.

\emph{Recovery procedures.} DynEGA (\citeproc{ref-glla2010}{Boker et
al., 2010}; \citeproc{ref-golino2019investigating}{H. Golino et al.,
2020}; \citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{H. Golino et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-golino2017ega1}{H. F. Golino \& Epskamp, 2017}) is the
method of primary interest. It estimates the first derivative of each
indicator from time-delay embeddings, estimates a regularized
partial-correlation network among the derivatives, and applies a
community-detection algorithm to the network; the communities are the
estimated dimensions. GraphicalVAR (\citeproc{ref-epskampggm}{Epskamp et
al., 2018}; \citeproc{ref-graphicalVAR2024}{Epskamp, 2024}) fits a
multilevel vector autoregression that separates lagged from
contemporaneous relations; its contemporaneous residual
partial-correlation network is used for grouping. GIMME
(\citeproc{ref-gates2012group}{Gates \& Molenaar, 2012};
\citeproc{ref-lane2019uncovering}{Lane et al., 2019}) fits a structural
vector autoregression to each clip separately; residual covariance
matrices from successful fits are averaged across clips, and the average
is used for grouping. This use of GIMME is computationally demanding and
differs from its intended idiographic application, so the GIMME results
apply to this comparison only.

Static EGA estimates a regularized partial-correlation network from the
contemporaneous correlation matrix of indicator levels, ignoring the
observation order, and applies the same community-detection step. It
serves two purposes. Statistically, it shows how much of the grouping is
recoverable from contemporaneous levels alone; if most of it is, a
dynamic model cannot add much for this recovery target. Computationally,
it requires far less time than the dynamic methods. Comparing static EGA
with DynEGA and GraphicalVAR therefore shows whether modeling temporal
structure improves grouping enough to justify its computational cost in
these conditions.

\emph{Primary design and outcomes.} The primary design uses \(T=100\)
observations per clip and contains 21,600 datasets. It crosses the six
scenario families, \(m\in\{2,3\}\) dimensions, \(N\in\{50,100\}\) clips,
three loading levels (0.40, 0.60, 0.80), three residual standard
deviations (0.125, 0.25, 0.50), and 100 replications, with \(k=4\)
indicators per dimension. Every method receives the same dataset within
a condition and replication. Because the MAFW segments have a median
length of 35 observations, the same 21,600 conditions were also run with
\(T=25\) and are reported as a secondary analysis. Louvain at resolution
1.0 is the primary community-detection algorithm; Leiden and Walktrap
were applied to the same fitted networks as sensitivity analyses.

The primary outcome is the overall correct-dimension recovery rate: the
proportion of datasets in which the method returned a valid network and
the estimated number of dimensions equaled \(m\). A failed or timed-out
fit counts as an unsuccessful recovery. We also report the valid-output
rate and the correct-dimension rate among valid outputs, so that
computational feasibility and accuracy can be examined separately.
Assignment accuracy, the adjusted Rand index, and variation of
information compare the full estimated partition of indicators with the
true partition among valid outputs. A secondary diagnostic summarizes
each recovered group by its first principal component and compares that
summary with the corresponding latent trajectory; these trajectory
diagnostics are reported in Appendix C and are not primary outcomes.

\emph{Additional analyses.} Three additional analyses examine the limits
of the primary result. First, one-at-a-time sensitivity analyses start
from a reference condition (\(N=100\), \(T=100\), loading 0.60, residual
SD 0.25, shared initial state) and change one feature: independent
rather than shared initial states, gradual rather than abrupt activity
transitions, small cross-loadings, residual correlation between
indicators, correlated acceleration inputs, or transformation of the
indicators to bounded scores. The bounded-score condition applies the
softmax function directly to the \(p\) continuous indicators and
analyzes either the \(p\) scores or their centered log-ratio (CLR)
components. Because the transformed scores must sum to one, the original
block structure among the \(p\) variables need not be preserved. Second,
a small analysis adds clip-specific baseline and scale differences, with
baselines constrained to the column space of the loading matrix so that
they add no structure outside the factor model. Third, an extended
factorial simulation of 518,400 datasets evaluates DynEGA alone over
additional numbers of dimensions, indicators per dimension, and
observation schedules. Appendix B gives the exact settings for every
analysis.

\subsection{Robustness and Sensitivity
Analyses}\label{robustness-and-sensitivity-analyses}

\emph{Shorter records.} With \(T=25\), static EGA had the highest
overall recovery, 0.913, followed by DynEGA, 0.863, and GraphicalVAR,
0.770. Shortening the record slightly reduced DynEGA's recovery, left
GraphicalVAR's about the same, and increased static EGA's. Under the
900-second limit, no GIMME fit at \(T=25\) returned a valid network.

\emph{Initial states.} In the reference condition of the one-at-a-time
analyses (\(N=100\), \(T=100\), loading 0.60, residual SD 0.25, shared
initial state), overall recovery was 0.993 for DynEGA, 0.912 for static
EGA, and 0.683 for GraphicalVAR. Replacing the shared initial state with
independent initial states produced the largest change of any
sensitivity condition: recovery fell to 0.838, 0.525, and 0.253,
respectively. A shared initial state therefore overstates recovery for
all three methods.

\emph{Other one-at-a-time changes.} Gradual activity transitions, small
cross-loadings, and correlated acceleration inputs changed recovery
little. A residual correlation of 0.10 between every pair of indicators
increased recovery for GraphicalVAR and static EGA, but because that
correlation also links indicators of different dimensions, the increase
should not be read as improved performance.

\emph{Bounded scores.} Transforming the \(p\) indicators to scores that
sum to one changed the variables being analyzed and sharply reduced
recovery of the original grouping. DynEGA recovered the correct number
of dimensions in 0.333 of datasets analyzed as CLR components and 0.233
analyzed as raw scores; the corresponding rates were 0.333 and 0.167 for
static EGA and 0.087 and 0.002 for GraphicalVAR. The softmax
transformation and the accompanying projection onto the zero-sum
subspace did not preserve the structure among the original indicators.
For this reason, the Study 2 recovery results apply to continuous
indicators and are not extended to bounded scores of the kind analyzed
in Study 1.

\emph{Between-clip baseline and scale.} This analysis used stable
families A--D with independent stationary initial states, so its
reference recovery rates are lower than in the shared-initial-state
analyses. Adding clip-specific baseline and scale changed DynEGA's
recovery from 0.775 to 0.750, a paired difference of \(-0.025\) (95\%
interval \([-0.100,0.050]\)). Static EGA rose from 0.275 to 1.000: the
added baselines lie in the column space of the loadings, so they add
between-clip variation that follows the intended grouping. GraphicalVAR
and GIMME were unchanged, but those estimates rest on very few valid
outputs; GraphicalVAR produced three correct outputs among 80 datasets,
and every three-dimensional GIMME fit timed out.

\emph{Runtime and feasibility.} Median runtime among valid \(T=100\)
outputs was 2.15 s for static EGA, 3.69 s for DynEGA, 85.41 s for
GraphicalVAR, and 518.16 s for GIMME. GraphicalVAR timed out on 1,271 of
21,600 fits. GIMME produced 4,846 valid outputs, 14,477 timeouts, and
2,277 post-estimation errors. DynEGA returned a valid output for every
fit in the primary and extended designs. These runtimes are specific to
our implementation and hardware. Appendix C gives the full runtime,
factorial, and community-detection results.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

LOMM addresses a common measurement problem in behavioral research. A
classifier can produce multivariate time series from video, and an
analysis can return dimensions or networks from those series, yet the
empirical data alone cannot reveal whether the intended structure was
recoverable. LOMM makes the recovery target explicit by specifying
latent dynamics, time-varying activity, and the observation model
separately. This separation is the paper's central contribution. It also
applies beyond video whenever intensive longitudinal measures are
treated as fallible indicators of lower-dimensional psychological
processes. CLIP is not part of LOMM; it is the scoring pipeline that
produced the empirical series in Study 1.

Study 1 asked whether LOMM output resembles the score series from that
pipeline. Letting clips differ in baseline and scale reduced the
composite discrepancy by 21.0\%, most of it from the baseline. This
result is specific to 100 MAFW clips, one label set, one scoring model,
and the chosen summary statistics, and because the held-out folds were
used both to compare and to select configurations, it is exploratory. It
supports a starting point for simulating such series; it does not
validate the emotion labels or LOMM as a substantive theory of
expression.

Study 2 evaluated recovery of a known factor structure from continuous
indicators. DynEGA had the highest overall recovery and was the only
method feasible in the extended factorial simulation. Static EGA showed
that most of the grouping was recoverable from contemporaneous levels;
it exceeded GraphicalVAR in both dimensional recovery and assignment
accuracy while running in a median of 2.15 s rather than 85.41 s. The
practical implication is that, for this recovery target and these
conditions, modeling temporal structure did not improve recovery enough
to offset its computational cost. GraphicalVAR answers additional
questions about lagged relations, which this comparison does not
evaluate.

The trajectory diagnostics in Appendix C show that the ranking of
methods depends on the recovery target. DynEGA's advantage concerned
recovery of the number of dimensions and the indicator partition;
alignment of recovered-group summaries with the latent trajectories
produced a different ordering. The two targets should be evaluated
separately. All comparisons apply to the specific estimation,
network-conversion, and grouping procedures used here. GIMME's low
overall recovery mainly reflected its failure to return valid outputs
under a clip-level fitting procedure that differs from its usual use.

The most important qualification of the primary result concerns initial
states. A shared starting state creates a shared initial transient
across dimensions, which adds recoverable structure that is not part of
the measurement partition. Independent initial states reduced recovery
for DynEGA, static EGA, and GraphicalVAR, so the shared-initial-state
design overstated recovery. Gradual activity transitions, small
cross-loadings, and correlated acceleration inputs changed little. The
bounded-score condition set a clearer limit: the softmax transformation
changed the associations among the original indicators, and neither the
raw scores nor the CLR components preserved the original grouping. The
Study 2 recovery claims therefore apply to continuous indicators.

\emph{Limitations.} Several limitations remain. The latent dimensions
are uncoupled. The amplifying families produce large amplitudes late in
the record and test recovery under those conditions only. The \(T=300\)
conditions also change the observation interval, so they do not isolate
the number of observations per clip. The GIMME residual-covariance
conversion was constructed for this comparison and differs from typical
idiographic use. Study 1 uses short clips from one controlled-access
dataset and one zero-shot scoring pipeline. LOMM is used here only as a
generator with prespecified parameters; estimating it from data would
require the identification constraints listed in Appendix A.

Three practical conclusions follow. Initial conditions can create
structure unrelated to the intended measurement partition. Transforming
indicators to bounded scores can change the structure among the analyzed
variables. Computational feasibility belongs in any recovery evaluation,
because a method that returns no output cannot recover the target.
Applied researchers should therefore specify the latent dynamics,
activity process, observation model, and recovery target before
interpreting an empirical network. LOMM supports this workflow and can
be extended to other scoring systems, coupled latent dimensions, and
recovery targets defined directly for bounded data.

\section{Appendix A: Complete Specification of
LOMM}\label{appendix-a-complete-specification-of-lomm}

This appendix gives the formal LOMM specification behind the conceptual
account in the main text. It is intended for readers who want to
reimplement the generator or examine its assumptions and identification
limits.

\subsection{A.1 The Latent Transition}\label{a.1-the-latent-transition}

Equation 1 gives the continuous-time oscillator. The implementation
advances its two-component state exactly over one observation interval
using

\[
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)=\exp(\mathbf{A}_f\Delta),
\qquad
\mathbf{A}_f=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
\eta_f & \zeta_f
\end{bmatrix}
\qquad
\text{and}
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)=\int_0^{\Delta}\exp(\mathbf{A}_f u)\mathbf{b}\,du,
\quad
\mathbf{b}=
\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
1
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

For the nonzero position-feedback values used in the simulation grids,
the input map has the equivalent exact form

\[
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)
=\mathbf{A}_f^{-1}\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)-\mathbf{I}_2\}\mathbf{b}.
\]

The integral definition remains valid when \(\mathbf{A}_f\) is singular,
including \(\eta_f=0\). If a root-based closed form is used numerically,
a separate repeated-root branch is required when \(\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f\)
is close to zero.

The state components \(x_{if,n}\) and \(v_{if,n}\) are the level and its
rate of change at \(t_n\); \(\eta_f\) is position feedback, \(\zeta_f\)
is velocity feedback, and \(\sigma_{qf}\) is the standard deviation of
the acceleration input. On the interval \([t_n,t_{n+1})\) the underlying
continuous-time model is
\(\ddot{x}_{if}(t)=\eta_f x_{if}(t)+\zeta_f\dot{x}_{if}(t)+q_{if,n}\)
with \(q_{if,n}\) held constant, so \(\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)\)
propagates the deterministic state and \(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)\)
loads the input. The inputs are independent across videos, dimensions,
and transitions, and independent of initial states, activity schedules,
and measurement errors. They are zero-order-hold acceleration inputs
rather than increments of a continuous-time white-noise diffusion. The
covariance added in one transition is
\(\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)\sigma_{qf}^{2}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)^\top\).

\subsection{A.2 Dynamic Regimes and Finite-Horizon
Amplification}\label{a.2-dynamic-regimes-and-finite-horizon-amplification}

The characteristic roots for dimension \(f\) are

\[
r_{f,\pm}=\frac{\zeta_f\pm\sqrt{\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f}}{2}.
\]

Continuous-time asymptotic stability requires \(\eta_f<0\) and
\(\zeta_f<0\), and the roots are oscillatory when
\(\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f<0\). The exact discrete transition is asymptotically
stable when

\[
\rho\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)\}
=\exp\!\left[\Delta\max\{\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,+}),\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,-})\}\right]<1.
\]

Negative \(\eta_f\) supplies restoring acceleration and negative
\(\zeta_f\) attenuates velocity, whereas positive \(\zeta_f\) produces
amplification. How much amplification actually appears depends on the
observed horizon. For \(T\) observations, \(H=(T-1)\Delta\). Let
\(\alpha(\mathbf{A}_f)=\max\{\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,+}),\operatorname{Re}(r_{f,-})\}\).
The quantity

\[
g_f(H)=\exp\{H\alpha(\mathbf{A}_f)\}
\]

is a modal exponential-rate factor over that horizon. It does not by
itself measure nonnormal transient amplification or the polynomial
factor that can occur at a defective repeated root. A norm-based
finite-horizon state-amplification diagnostic is

\[
G_f^{\max}(H)
=
\sup_{0\leq u\leq H}\|\exp(\mathbf{A}_fu)\|_2,
\]

and the corresponding level-only diagnostic is

\[
G_{x,f}^{\max}(H)
=
\sup_{0\leq u\leq H}
\left\|
\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}
\exp(\mathbf{A}_fu)
\right\|_2.
\]

The Euclidean matrix norm is coordinate-dependent. Because the state
combines a level and a rate of change, whose components have different
units, this diagnostic is conditional on the chosen state coordinates
and should be interpreted only after fixing a nondimensionalization or
an explicit weighted state norm. A coordinate-aware definition is

\[
G_{f,M}^{\max}(H)
=
\sup_{0\leq u\leq H}
\left\|
\mathbf{M}^{1/2}\exp(\mathbf{A}_fu)\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}
\right\|_2,
\]

for a stated positive-definite state metric \(\mathbf{M}\); the
unweighted \(G_f^{\max}(H)\) corresponds to \(\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{I}_2\).
Reporting the roots and \(g_f(H)\) characterizes the modal regime over
the observed horizon, but \(g_f(H)\) should not be read as total
finite-horizon amplification. Reporting the activity schedule separately
keeps amplification in the latent dynamics distinct from nonstationarity
created by the activity layer.

\subsection{A.3 Initial States}\label{a.3-initial-states}

The system is uncoupled: \(\eta_f\), \(\zeta_f\), and \(\sigma_{qf}\)
may vary by dimension, and off-diagonal dynamic effects are omitted.
Study 2's primary simulation design initialized every dimension at
\(x_{if,1}=0.25\) and \(v_{if,1}=2\). The sensitivity analysis used
independent starting states. For a stable dimension, the stationary
covariance \(\mathbf P_f\) solves

\[
\mathbf{P}_f=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)\mathbf{P}_f\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)^\top
+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)\sigma_{qf}^2\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_f(\Delta)^\top,
\]

whose solution is unique when \(\rho\{\boldsymbol{\Phi}_f(\Delta)\}<1\).
Drawing \(\mathbf{s}_{if,1}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{P}_f)\)
makes the state process stationary before the activity layer is applied;
time-varying activity modulation still renders \(a_{if}(t)x_{if}(t)\)
nonstationary. For a dimension with positive velocity feedback, the
sensitivity draws \(x_{if,1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-0.25,0.25)\) and
\(v_{if,1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(-2,2)\). These independent starts remove the
transient that all dimensions would otherwise share, while leaving the
observed horizon unchanged.

\subsection{A.4 The Activity Layer at a
Transition}\label{a.4-the-activity-layer-at-a-transition}

Away from a discontinuity in the activity value, the ordinary product
rule applies,

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\{a_{if}(t)x_{if}(t)\}
=a_{if}'(t)x_{if}(t)+a_{if}(t)x_{if}'(t).
\]

At an abrupt transition, the derivative of the activity value contains
an impulsive component in the distributional sense. Define
\(D_nx=x_{n+1}-x_n\). The exact discrete identity is

\[
D_n(a_{if}x_{if})
=
a_{if,n+1}D_nx_{if}
+
(D_na_{if})x_{if,n},
\]

where the first term uses the post-transition activity value and the
second uses the pre-transition state. The corresponding
finite-difference identity is

\[
\frac{\tilde{x}_{if,n+1}-\tilde{x}_{if,n}}{\Delta}
=a_{if,n+1}\frac{x_{if,n+1}-x_{if,n}}{\Delta}
+\frac{a_{if,n+1}-a_{if,n}}{\Delta}x_{if,n}.
\]

The second term is the term that enters an estimated derivative at an
activity switch even when the oscillator itself has not accelerated;
this motivates the gradual-transition sensitivity analysis. This
convention attaches the new activity value to the post-transition
observation, so the activity layer multiplies each completed latent
state by the schedule value at the same observation index.

The activity schedule takes two nonnegative values,
\(a_{\mathrm{high}}\) and \(a_{\mathrm{low}}\). In non-switching
families the dominant dimension remains at \(a_{\mathrm{high}}\) for the
whole record and the others remain at \(a_{\mathrm{low}}\). In switching
families, dwell fractions determine how long each dimension remains
dominant before the schedule reassigns \(a_{\mathrm{high}}\). Appendix B
lists the numeric gains and dwell values used in the Study 2 families.

\subsection{A.5 The Observation Layer}\label{a.5-the-observation-layer}

Let \(J\) denote the number of positive score channels, let \(d=J-1\),
and let \(\mathbf V\in\mathbb R^{J\times d}\) be a fixed orthonormal
isometric log-ratio (ILR) basis satisfying
\(\mathbf V^\top\mathbf V=\mathbf I_d\) and
\(\mathbf V^\top\mathbf 1_J=\mathbf 0\). The Study 1 basis used here is
a normalized Helmert basis constructed in the stated 11-channel order.
The full observation model in log-ratio coordinates is

\[
\mathbf u_{in}
=
\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b_i
+\kappa s_i
\left(
\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{in}
+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}
\right).
\]

Here \(\boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb R^d\) is a fixed center,
\(\mathbf b_i\in\mathbb R^d\) is the clip baseline, \(s_i>0\) is the
clip scale, \(\kappa>0\) is a global scale, and
\(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\in\mathbb R^{d\times m}\) maps the \(m\)
activity-scaled latent positions into these coordinates. The clip scale
multiplies both signal and residual, whereas the baseline is additive.
Conditional on the clip-specific terms and \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\),
measurement errors are independent over time and across clips, and
independent of latent states, initial states, activity schedules, and
acceleration inputs, with
\(\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}\mid\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\boldsymbol\Sigma_i)\).
Dependence across coordinates follows \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\). Designs
with fixed measurement parameters set
\(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i=\boldsymbol\Lambda\) and
\(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i=\boldsymbol\Sigma\) for every clip. The
residual-correlation sensitivity condition sets
\(\boldsymbol\Sigma=s_\varepsilon^2\{(1-\rho)\mathbf I_p+\rho\mathbf 1_p\mathbf 1_p^\top\}\)
with \(\rho=0.10\) across all \(p\) indicators, thereby introducing
residual covariance both within and between the prespecified blocks.

The three baseline constructions differ only in the subspace from which
\(\mathbf b_i\) is drawn. Study 1 draws it from a zero-mean Gaussian
whose covariance is estimated from training clips in the full coordinate
space. Study 2's between-clip-variability analysis instead uses

\[
\mathbf b_i=\boldsymbol\Lambda\boldsymbol\beta_i,
\qquad E(\boldsymbol\beta_i)=\mathbf 0,
\]

so that between-clip variation stays within the space spanned by the
loadings and does not introduce structure outside the factor model.
Study 2 conditions without baseline and scale differences fix
\(\mathbf b_i=\mathbf 0\) and \(s_i=1\), giving

\[
\mathbf u_{in}
=\boldsymbol\mu
+\kappa\left(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{in}
+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in}\right).
\]

In the matched-method and extended factorial designs,
\(\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\) and the diagonal of \(\boldsymbol\Sigma_i\)
receive small clip-specific perturbations to the loadings and residual
variances. The one-at-a-time sensitivity and between-clip-variability
designs use fixed equal values except where the stated condition changes
them. Thus \(\mathbf b_i=\mathbf 0\) and \(s_i=1\) remove baseline and
overall-scale differences; they do not by themselves imply a common
loading matrix or residual covariance.

The continuous indicators used in Study 2's primary recovery designs are
the indicator vector before any bounded-score mapping,

\[
\mathbf z_{in}=\boldsymbol\Lambda_i\tilde{\mathbf x}_{in}+\boldsymbol\varepsilon_{in},
\]

in which each of the \(p=mk\) indicators loads on one dimension and has
diagonal measurement error.

For bounded output, LOMM maps the coordinates to the open probability
simplex. The centered log-ratio (CLR) expresses each channel relative to
the geometric mean of all channels. The isometric log-ratio (ILR)
represents the same information in \(d=J-1\) orthonormal coordinates.
Formally,

\[
\operatorname{clr}(\mathbf p)
=\log\mathbf p-
J^{-1}\{\mathbf 1_J^\top\log(\mathbf p)\}\mathbf 1_J,
\qquad
\operatorname{ilr}_{\mathbf V}(\mathbf p)
=\mathbf V^\top\operatorname{clr}(\mathbf p),
\]

with the logarithm applied componentwise. For the canonical bounded LOMM
output, applying softmax to \(\mathbf V\mathbf u_{in}/\tau\) gives

\[
\operatorname{ilr}_{\mathbf V}(\mathbf p_{in})=\frac{\mathbf u_{in}}{\tau},
\qquad
\operatorname{clr}(\mathbf p_{in})
=\frac{\mathbf V\mathbf u_{in}}{\tau},
\]

the second because \(\mathbf V\mathbf u_{in}\) lies in the zero-sum
log-ratio subspace. The fixed basis excludes the unidentified
common-logit direction and the softmax produces strictly positive rows
summing to one, so the scores identify the scaled coordinates
\(\mathbf u_{in}/\tau\) exactly. A common-logit component remains
unobservable, and the unscaled coordinates and \(\tau\) are not
separately identified without an external scale constraint.

The Study 2 bounded-score sensitivity condition uses the \(p=mk\)
continuous-indicator vector directly. With \(J=p\), it computes

\[
\mathbf p^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}
=\operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf z_{in}}{\tau}\right),
\qquad
\operatorname{clr}\!\left(\mathbf p^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}\right)
=\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbf P_p\mathbf z_{in},
\qquad
\mathbf P_p
=\mathbf I_p-\frac{\mathbf 1_p\mathbf 1_p^\top}{p}.
\]

For any orthonormal ILR basis
\(\mathbf V_p\in\mathbb R^{p\times(p-1)}\),
\(\mathbf V_p\mathbf V_p^\top=\mathbf P_p\). Defining
\(\mathbf u^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}=\mathbf V_p^\top\mathbf z_{in}\)
therefore gives
\(\mathbf V_p\mathbf u^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}=\mathbf P_p\mathbf z_{in}\).
Softmax is invariant to the removed row mean, so the direct-softmax
branch is equivalent to applying the basis form to
\(\mathbf u^{\mathrm{dep}}_{in}\). The analysis used the \(p\) raw
scores and the \(p\) CLR components, which satisfy a zero-sum
constraint; it did not analyze the \((p-1)\) ILR coordinates. The
common-logit direction in \(\mathbf z_{in}\) is unidentifiable after
this transformation.

\subsection{A.6 Converting Period and Damping to Feedback
Parameters}\label{a.6-converting-period-and-damping-to-feedback-parameters}

Study 1's search was parameterized by natural period and damping ratio
rather than by \(\eta_f\) and \(\zeta_f\) directly. If \(P_f\) is the
natural period in observation intervals and \(\xi_f\) is the damping
ratio, then

\[
\omega_{n,f}=\frac{2\pi}{P_f\Delta},
\qquad
\eta_f=-\omega_{n,f}^2,
\qquad
\zeta_f=-2\xi_f\omega_{n,f},
\]

and the damped angular frequency is
\(\omega_{d,f}=\omega_{n,f}\sqrt{1-\xi_f^2}\). With \(P_f=12\),
\(\xi_f=0.5\), and \(\Delta=1\), these give \(\eta_f=-0.2742\) and
\(\zeta_f=-0.5236\); scaling the inputs to unit stationary position
variance gives \(\sigma_{qf}=0.5418\).

\subsection{A.7 Identifiability}\label{a.7-identifiability}

For any nonsingular diagonal matrix \(\mathbf{D}\),

\[
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{in}
=(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{D}^{-1})(\mathbf{D}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{in}),
\]

so loading magnitude and latent-trajectory scale are not jointly
identified without an explicit scale constraint. Activity gain and
acceleration-input scale contribute to latent amplitude in the same way.
The softmax layer adds invariance to a common location shift and a
tradeoff between the temperature and the coordinate scale. Dimension
labels are recoverable only up to permutation.

Sampling cadence adds an aliasing restriction. For an underdamped
dimension, write

\[
r_{f,\pm}=\frac{\zeta_f}{2}\pm i\omega_f,
\qquad
\omega_f=
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{-(\zeta_f^2+4\eta_f)}.
\]

At observation times \(t_n=(n-1)\Delta\), the frequencies \(\omega_f\)
and \(\omega_f+2\pi q/\Delta\) for integer \(q\) generate the same
sampled sine and cosine sequences after an appropriate change in initial
velocity. Identifying continuous-time oscillator parameters from data
therefore requires a cadence-dependent restriction such as

\[
0<\omega_f<\frac{\pi}{\Delta},
\]

together with a specified acceleration-input model and constraints on
the latent state basis. Estimating LOMM from data would in total require
sign and scale constraints on the dimensions, normalization of the
activity schedule, anchoring of either the loading scale or the input
scale, a reference-logit or sum-to-zero constraint, a fixed or otherwise
identified temperature, and this frequency restriction. Every
oscillatory mode used here satisfies the restriction: Study 1 uses
\(\Delta=1\), giving the most restrictive bound \(\pi\approx3.14\)
against a largest angular frequency of approximately 0.78, and Study 2
uses \(\Delta\in\{0.25,0.4\}\), giving bounds of approximately 12.57 and
7.85 against a largest angular frequency of approximately 1.04. The
simulations do not estimate any of these parameters.

\section{Appendix B: Study Designs and Implementation
Details}\label{appendix-b-study-designs-and-implementation-details}

This appendix gives the design and implementation details for both
studies. Study 1 evaluates resemblance to real bounded-score series.
Study 2 evaluates recovery of a prespecified factor structure from
continuous indicators.

\subsection{B.1 Study 1 Scoring and
Calibration}\label{b.1-study-1-scoring-and-calibration}

The Study 1 sample was assembled one clip at a time, with MAFW category
labels used to diversify the sample rather than as validation targets.
The main analysis contains 3,557 observations in 188 uninterrupted
segments from 100 clips. Segment lengths range from 16 to 50
observations, with a median of 35. The gap-interpolated sensitivity
analysis contains 3,632 observations in 129 segments, with lengths from
17 to 50 and a median of 36.5.

transforEmotion sampled up to 50 frames uniformly from each video and
used the OpenAI CLIP ViT-Large/14 model family. A Haar cascade detected
faces, and the largest detected face was expanded by 50 pixels on each
side before classification. Frames without a detected face received
missing scores. Eleven lowercase class labels were passed directly to
the text encoder without a sentence template: anger, disgust, fear,
happiness, neutral, sadness, surprise, contempt, anxiety, helplessness,
and disappointment. Burned-in subtitles were not masked and could
contribute when they fell inside the padded face crop.

Scores were required to lie in \([0,1]\). A complete row was retained
when its sum was positive, finite, and within \(10^{-6}\) of one, then
divided by that sum in the fixed channel order. Values were not
truncated and zero replacement was disabled. The main analysis retained
complete rows. The sensitivity analysis interpolated internal gaps of at
most two frames within a segment. The scoring-time package release and
exact checkpoint revision were not archived, so exact frame rescoring
cannot be guaranteed; all analyses use the retained scores.

The comparison used eight statistics. Median level, interquartile range
of levels, interquartile range of observation-to-observation changes,
lag-1 autocorrelation, entropy of monotonic run lengths, and normalized
spectral entropy were calculated for each channel. Effective rank and
total variance described all channels jointly. Each statistic was
evaluated on both bounded scores and log-ratio coordinates.

For every statistic and representation, disagreement between simulated
and real clip distributions was measured by the absolute difference
between medians and the one-dimensional Wasserstein distance. Both were
standardized by the spread of the real values, using the interquartile
range when available, followed by \(1.4826\) times the median absolute
deviation, the standard deviation, or one. The larger standardized
distance was retained. The composite averaged the eight statistics
within each representation and weighted the two representations equally.

Clips were divided into five deterministic folds within MAFW category
and length order, leaving 20 test clips per fold. The coordinate center,
loading directions, loading scales, and covariance of clip baselines
were estimated from training clips only. The first three eigenvectors of
the within-clip covariance supplied the loading directions. Their
orientation and scale were fixed deterministically. The baseline
covariance was shrunk halfway toward its diagonal and eigenvalues were
floored at \(10^{-8}\).

Within each fold and configuration, the search crossed natural periods
\(\{8,12,24\}\), damping ratios \(\{0.05,0.20,0.50\}\), and observation
scales \(\kappa\in\{0.75,1.00,1.25\}\). Three paired training datasets
evaluated each of the 27 candidates. Selection minimized the equally
weighted representation-level discrepancy, with deterministic
tie-breaking. All folds selected a 12-observation period, damping ratio
0.50, and \(\kappa=1\) for the clip baseline + scale configuration.

Each selected configuration generated 100 datasets per test fold,
matching the held-out observation indices and segment structure. The
comparison concerns distributions of summary statistics across clips,
rather than frame-level reconstruction. Uncertainty used 2,000 paired
bootstrap resamples of the 20 clips within each fold. The eight
configurations are the factorial combinations of clip baseline, clip
scale, and brief activity transition.

\subsection{B.3 Method
Implementations}\label{b.3-method-implementations}

DynEGA estimates first derivatives from time-delay embeddings,
constructs an EBICglasso partial-correlation network, and groups its
positive edges (\citeproc{ref-glla2010}{Boker et al., 2010};
\citeproc{ref-golino2019investigating}{H. Golino et al., 2020};
\citeproc{ref-golino2020modeling}{H. Golino et al., 2022};
\citeproc{ref-golino2017ega1}{H. F. Golino \& Epskamp, 2017}). Video
identity prevents embedding windows from crossing clip boundaries. An
explicit search selects the embedding dimension by the Total Entropy Fit
Index, with shorter embeddings breaking ties. Candidate dimensions are
\(\{5,10,15,25,50\}\) for \(T=100\) and \(\{5,6,10,12\}\) for \(T=25\).
EGAnet 2.3.0 uses GLLA embedding parameters tau = 1 and delta = 1, first
derivatives, automatic correlations, pairwise missing-data handling, and
the glasso model. The derivative scale does not affect the correlation
matrix because conversion from observation units to physical time
multiplies every derivative by the same positive constant.

Static EGA applies EBICglasso to the contemporaneous correlation matrix
of the stacked indicator levels and then uses the same
network-processing and community-detection rules as DynEGA. It ignores
the observation order. This makes it a benchmark that shows how much of
the grouping is recoverable without temporal information, and a
computational benchmark for the temporal procedures.

GraphicalVAR uses mlGraphicalVAR with clip identity as the grouping
variable, within-clip centering, variable standardization, and gamma 0.5
(\citeproc{ref-epskampggm}{Epskamp et al., 2018};
\citeproc{ref-graphicalVAR2024}{Epskamp, 2024}). Lagged pairs never
cross clip boundaries. The grouping uses the returned fixedPCC
contemporaneous residual partial-correlation matrix. The diagonal is
removed and the matrix is treated as an undirected weighted graph.

GIMME fits each standardized clip separately with unified structural
equation modeling and vector autoregression
(\citeproc{ref-gates2012group}{Gates \& Molenaar, 2012};
\citeproc{ref-lane2019uncovering}{Lane et al., 2019}). Standardized
residual covariance matrices from successful fits are aligned by
variable name, averaged across clips, and symmetrized. The resulting
graph is a residual-covariance graph. Unequal residual variances can
therefore influence its weights. This conversion was built for the
present comparison and differs from typical idiographic use.

For every method, negative graph weights are set to zero. If no positive
edge remains, absolute values of the original off-diagonal weights are
used; only an all-zero matrix produces singleton communities. The
integrated outputs do not record the frequency of this fallback. Louvain
at resolution 1.0 is primary (\citeproc{ref-blondel2008fast}{Blondel et
al., 2008}). Leiden and Walktrap are applied to the same fitted network
as sensitivity analyses (\citeproc{ref-pons2006walktrap}{Pons \& Latapy,
2005}; \citeproc{ref-traag2019leiden}{Traag et al., 2019}). A
deterministic offset from the dataset seed initializes each method fit.

GraphicalVAR and GIMME were omitted from the 518,400-dataset extended
factorial simulation because the full design was computationally
impractical. Both were included in the primary and shorter-series
designs. GraphicalVAR was also included in the one-at-a-time sensitivity
and between-clip analyses. GIMME was included in the between-clip
analysis and excluded from the one-at-a-time sensitivity analyses.

\subsection{B.4 Outcomes, Uncertainty, and
Computing}\label{b.4-outcomes-uncertainty-and-computing}

The valid-output rate records whether a procedure returned a network
that could be grouped. The conditional correct-dimension rate records
correct dimensionality among valid outputs. The primary outcome, the
overall correct-dimension recovery rate, requires both events, so errors
and timeouts remain in its denominator.

Adjusted Rand index and variation of information compare the full
estimated and prespecified partitions among valid outputs. Assignment
accuracy matches estimated groups to dimensions to maximize correct
indicator assignments. Every indicator remains in the denominator, and
indicators in unmatched groups count as incorrect.

Trajectory-summary diagnostics are constructed after grouping.
Indicators are standardized and each estimated group is summarized by
its first principal component, with a row mean used when a component
cannot be computed. Correlation pairs each prespecified dimension with
the estimated summary having the largest absolute correlation. NRMSE
uses one-to-one matching after sign reversal when needed. An unmatched
data-generating dimension receives the penalty 2. All-attempt
failure-adjusted versions assign correlation 0 and NRMSE 2 when no valid
network output exists.

Uncertainty uses 2,000 bootstrap resamples of whole datasets, stratified
by condition. Monte Carlo standard errors use delete-one-dataset
jackknifing. The three community-detection algorithms reuse the same
fitted structure and therefore assess the grouping step rather than
estimator variability.

Analyses used R 4.5.1 on Rocky Linux 8.8 with dual Intel Xeon Silver
4310 processors, 48 logical threads, and 125 GiB RAM. Key versions were
EGAnet 2.3.0, graphicalVAR 0.3.4, gimme 10.0, igraph 2.2.1, qgraph
1.9.8, psych 2.5.6, lavaan 0.6-20, dplyr 1.1.4, and ggplot2 4.0.1.
GraphicalVAR and GIMME used a 900-second limit per fit in the primary
and shorter designs.

Runtime is elapsed wall-clock time for attempts returning a valid
network output. It includes structure estimation, network extraction,
and community detection. One fitted structure is reused for Louvain,
Leiden, and Walktrap, so feasibility counts unique fits and accuracy
counts fit-and-algorithm results. Runtime values are specific to this
implementation and machine.
