\begin{center}
{\Large Response to the Editor and Reviewer}\\[0.5em]
Manuscript: \textit{A Latent Oscillator Measurement Model to Simulate Emotional-Expression Score Dynamics in Video}
\end{center}

Dear Dr. Epskamp, Editors, and Reviewer,

Thank you for the careful reading and for the guidance on scope, structure, and examples. We revised the paper as a methods contribution centered on the Latent Oscillator Measurement Model (LOMM). The main changes are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Name and scope.} We renamed the model from DLO-MM to LOMM because damping is one of three velocity-feedback regimes the model allows (damped, undamped, amplifying). This fits short video time series better, because brief expressive episodes in the MAFW clips may show bursts, switching, or low expressivity without clear damping. The paper now has one aim: LOMM generates data with a known factor structure so that analysis methods can be tested for whether they recover that structure.
  \item \textit{Structure.} The manuscript has six main sections (Introduction, LOMM, Study 1, Study 2, Results, Discussion) and a pipeline figure (Figure 1). The full model specification is in Appendix A, design and implementation details in Appendix B, and supplementary results in Appendix C (Study 1 diagnostics, grouping and trajectory diagnostics, one-at-a-time sensitivity analyses, the extended factorial simulation, runtime and feasibility, and community-detection sensitivity).
  \item \textit{Realism.} A new Study 1 compares simulated score series with classifier scores from 100 MAFW clips on eight summary statistics, using five-fold cross-fitting. MAFW is defined at its first mention in the Introduction.
  \item \textit{Benchmarks and metrics.} Study 2 now compares DynEGA with static EGA, GraphicalVAR, and GIMME on identical datasets. It reports recovery with failures and timeouts counted as incorrect, adds assignment accuracy, adjusted Rand index, variation of information, and NRMSE, and reports runtime. Its primary analyses use continuous indicators generated by LOMM; all clips share the same indicator assignments and nominal loadings and residual variances, with small clip-specific perturbations. A separate sensitivity condition applies the softmax directly to the \(p\) continuous indicators and analyzes the resulting bounded scores and their centered log-ratio (CLR) components; the original grouping is not preserved, which is why the Study 2 recovery claims are restricted to continuous indicators.
  \item \textit{Examples.} The political example is replaced with a non-political MAFW clip.
  \item \textit{Tutorial material.} Removed, because the \textit{transforEmotion} tutorial is published separately and is cited in Study 1.
\end{enumerate}

Because line numbers change during production, we refer below to sections, figures, tables, and page numbers in the revised PDF.

  \item \point{Realism of LOMM simulations}
  \begin{reviewquote}

  \response We added a simulated-versus-real comparison using MAFW-derived classifier-score time series. Study 1 evaluates eight summary statistics: median level, interquartile range of the level, interquartile range of frame-to-frame changes, lag-1 autocorrelation, entropy of monotonic run lengths, normalized spectral entropy, effective rank, and total variance. Each statistic is evaluated on the scores and on their log-ratio coordinates, giving 16 comparisons across the retained 100 video clips. The analysis is a five-fold cross-fitted exploratory comparison of eight configurations. The same held-out folds were used to compare the configurations and identify the lowest-discrepancy one, so the comparison is descriptive; an unbiased external assessment would require new data or an outer validation split. Clip baseline produced most of the discrepancy reduction; clip scale contributed less. Study 2 is a separate analysis and is described under the next point.

  \item \point{Alternative dimensionality recovery benchmarks}
  \begin{reviewquote}
  I appreciate the effort in developing a simulation framework, which is not common in image classification work as far as I am aware.   \end{reviewquote}

  \response We added static EGA, GraphicalVAR, and GIMME to the matched-method simulation design. Static EGA ignores temporal order and is computationally inexpensive; it shows how much of the grouping is recoverable from contemporaneous indicator levels alone. GraphicalVAR is the dynamic network comparison. GIMME is included as a per-clip method whose residual-covariance matrices from valid clip-level fits are averaged, symmetrized, converted to a nonnegative graph by the prespecified rule, and partitioned; this tests computational feasibility as well as recovery.

  The main Results report the valid-output rate, the correct-dimension rate among valid outputs, and the overall correct-dimension recovery rate. The last quantity counts every failure and timeout as an unsuccessful recovery. Assignment accuracy is reported among valid network outputs, and runtime and feasibility are counted per unique fitted structure. Appendix C reports the complete grouping diagnostics, trajectory correlation and NRMSE, and all-attempt failure-adjusted trajectory measures; an attempt without a valid network output receives correlation 0 and NRMSE 2.

  The comparison shows that recovery was not specific to DynEGA. GraphicalVAR and GIMME are included in the matched-method design, and the comparison applies to the procedures and conditions used there. At \(T=100\), overall correct-dimension recovery was 0.939 for DynEGA, 0.884 for static EGA, 0.777 for GraphicalVAR, and 0.176 for GIMME. Static EGA also exceeded GraphicalVAR in assignment accuracy and required a median 2.15 seconds rather than 85.41 seconds. This result makes clear how much of the target was recoverable from contemporaneous structure. Appendix B documents the exact estimator and graph-conversion procedures, including the GIMME conversion used here.
